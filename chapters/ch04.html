Determinants, invertibility, and volume

L11 L12

The third channel in Chapter 2 repeats the sum of the first two. Suppose the instrument now measures three actuator settings with a small additional sensitivity \(\epsilon\) to the third setting. Can the readings determine all three settings? The determinant detects whether the image collapses to a lower-dimensional subspace. Its magnitude also measures volume scaling, but the propagation of measurement error depends on the direction of the error.

Cofactors and the signed expansion

L11 01:30

For a square matrix \(A=(a_{ij})\) over \(\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\), the minor associated with entry \(a_{ij}\) is the determinant of the matrix \(A_{ij}\) obtained by deleting row \(i\) and column \(j\). The cofactor is \(C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}\det A_{ij}\). Define the determinant recursively. The empty determinant is one. For every positive order, sum \(a_{1j}C_{1j}\) over the first row. In order one this gives the single entry.

The determinant can be computed by expansion along any row \(i\) or column \(j\):

\[\det A=\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}C_{ij} =\sum_{i=1}^n a_{ij}C_{ij}, \tag{4.1}\]

where \(i\) is fixed in the first sum and \(j\) in the second. To see the agreement, define a permutation \(\sigma\) of \(\{1,\ldots,n\}\) as a bijection of that set. Write \(\operatorname{sgn}\sigma=(-1)^k\), where \(k\) counts pairs \(i<j\) for which \(\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)\). The signed expansion is

\[\det A=\sum_{\sigma}\operatorname{sgn}\sigma \prod_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(i)}. \tag{4.2}\]

Group the terms by their selected column \(j\) in the fixed row \(i\). Moving row \(i\) and column \(j\) to the first positions takes \((i-1)+(j-1)\) adjacent swaps. Each adjacent swap reverses permutation parity, so the sign from these moves is \((-1)^{i+j}\). The remaining term is the signed permutation product in \(A_{ij}\). This gives the row formula and also proves agreement with the recursive definition by induction. Grouping by the selected row in a fixed column gives the column formula.

For the original dimensionless calibration extension use

\[A_\epsilon=\begin{pmatrix} 1&0&1\\ 0&1&1\\ 1&1&2+\epsilon \end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.3}\]

The first row expansion gives \((2+\epsilon-1)+(0-1)=\epsilon\). Each of the two contributions can have magnitude close to one even when their sum is small. For this example, removing the duplicate contribution from rows one and two before applying the determinant exposes the small quantity directly.

Elimination, multiplicativity, and singularity

L12 02:00

Every term in (4.2) uses one entry from each row and column. Therefore the determinant is linear in each row separately and in each column separately. Swapping two rows or two columns reverses the sign because it composes the permutation indexing of terms with a transposition. A matrix with two equal rows or columns thus has determinant zero. Scaling a row by a scalar scales the determinant by that scalar. Adding a multiple of one row to a different row preserves it, since the extra linear term has repeated rows. In an upper triangular matrix the identity permutation is the only one that can contribute. Every other permutation has some \(\sigma(i)<i\), selecting a zero below the diagonal.

Subtract rows one and two from row three of (4.3). The result has diagonal \(1,1,\epsilon\) and is upper triangular, again giving \(\det A_\epsilon=\epsilon\). In general, elimination that uses row interchanges and additions gives the signed product of the resulting diagonal entries. If row scaling is used, the determinant must also be corrected for those scale factors. A square matrix with full rank has a nonzero pivot in each column after suitable row exchanges. It therefore has nonzero determinant. A rank-deficient square matrix reduces to a form with a zero row and determinant zero. This proves the invertibility criterion. LAPACK's partial-pivoting factorization stores the row swaps and the triangular factors, so one can compute the signed diagonal product from those factors. LAPACK DGETRF documentation

For square matrices \(A,B\) of the same size, the columns of \(AB\) are combinations of columns of \(A\) with coefficients taken from \(B\). Expand the determinant of these combinations by multilinearity. A term with two equal selected columns of \(A\) is zero. A term with distinct columns equals \(\det A\) times the sign of their permutation, multiplied by its coefficient product from \(B\). The sum of those signed products is \(\det B\). Thus \(\det(AB)=\det A\,\det B\). Taking \(B=A^{-1}\) gives \(\det A^{-1}=1/\det A\) when \(A\) is invertible. Taking \(\sigma^{-1}\) in (4.2), which has the same sign as \(\sigma\), proves \(\det A^T=\det A\). Combining transpose with entrywise conjugation gives \(\det A^*=\overline{\det A}\). For an invertible basis change \(S\), multiplicativity gives \(\det(S^{-1}AS)=\det A\). L12 03:30

Volume and the adjugate

L12 14:00

Over \(\mathbb R\), the magnitude \(|\det A|\) is the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by its columns. For independent columns, Gram–Schmidt gives \(A=QR\) with square orthogonal \(Q\) and upper triangular \(R\) with positive diagonal. The transformation by \(Q\) preserves lengths and volumes. In the coordinates of \(Q\), each diagonal \(r_{jj}\) is the perpendicular height that column \(j\) adds to the previous span. The volume is the product of these heights by successive base-times-height calculations. Multiplicativity and \(\det(Q^TQ)=1\) give \(|\det A|=\prod_jr_{jj}\). If the columns are dependent the parallelepiped lies in a proper subspace and has zero full-dimensional volume. Row three of (4.3) adds only a small independent component when \(|\epsilon|\) is small, and the absolute determinant gives the volume of the transformed unit cube.

The adjugate \(\operatorname{adj}(A)\) is the transpose of the cofactor array. Thus \(\operatorname{adj}(A)_{kj}=C_{jk}\). The \((i,j)\) entry of \(A\operatorname{adj}(A)\) is \(\sum_k a_{ik}C_{jk}\). For \(i=j\), it is the expansion along row \(j\). For \(i\ne j\), it is the determinant expansion of a matrix with row \(j\) replaced by row \(i\). The minors in that expansion are unchanged because they omit row \(j\), and the repeated row makes the determinant zero. Consequently

\[A\operatorname{adj}(A)=(\det A)I_n,\qquad A^{-1}=\frac{\operatorname{adj}(A)}{\det A}\quad(\det A\ne0). \tag{4.4}\]

The cofactor transpose is called the adjoint in the recording. Here the name adjugate distinguishes it from the conjugate transpose \(A^*\). For example, the adjugate of \(\operatorname{diag}(2,3)\) is \(\operatorname{diag}(3,2)\), whereas its conjugate transpose is \(\operatorname{diag}(2,3)\). L12 16:46

The inverse relation for the sensor has a direct derivation. If \(A_\epsilon x=b\), then \(\epsilon x_3=b_3-b_1-b_2\) and \(x_1=b_1-x_3\), \(x_2=b_2-x_3\). For \(b=(2,1,3)^T\), the solution at nonzero \(\epsilon\) is \((2,1,0)^T\). A third-channel error \(\delta\) changes it by \(\delta(-1,-1,1)^T/\epsilon\). With \(\epsilon=10^{-3}\) and \(\delta=10^{-4}\) the exact settings change by \((-0.1,-0.1,0.1)^T\). An error \((\delta,0,\delta)^T\) instead leaves \(x_3\) unchanged and changes only \(x_1\) by \(\delta\). An invertibility decision and a sensitivity estimate answer different questions.

Laboratory

Run or inspect the Python laboratory. It takes no input and writes only to standard output. An exact signed-permutation calculation verifies the determinant for rational parameters, and a NumPy solve verifies both perturbation directions. A failed identity raises an exception. It also checks that a rescaling by a nonzero scalar preserves the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value even when a direct determinant underflows to zero in floating point.

For \(10^{-200}\operatorname{diag}(2,1)\) this run returns a direct determinant of zero and a natural logarithm of its magnitude of approximately \(-920.341\). The exact determinant is \(2\times10^{-400}\), which is positive and below the representable range of the floating-point type.

Exercises with worked solutions

Track elimination signs

Compute \(\det\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\) by elimination and by first-row expansion.

Solution. One row swap yields an upper triangular matrix with diagonal \((3,2)\), hence the original determinant is \(-6\). The first-row cofactor contribution is \(2(-1)^3\cdot3=-6\). A pivot rule can select row two, but the parity of that swap matters when recovering the determinant.

Compute the adjugate at singularity

Find \(\operatorname{adj}(A)\) for \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}\) and test (4.4).

Solution. The cofactor formula gives \(\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}\). Its columns are multiples of \((-2,1)^T\), which \(A\) sends to zero. Thus \(A\operatorname{adj}(A)=0=(\det A)I_2\) even though the adjugate is nonzero and division by the determinant is undefined.

Separate determinant from worst-direction gain

Compare \(I_2\) with \(D=\operatorname{diag}(10^{6},10^{-6})\) for equal-length coordinate perturbations in \(Dx=b\).

Solution. Both determinants equal one. Under \(I_2^{-1}\), either coordinate perturbation stays the same length. Under \(D^{-1}\), an error in reading one is attenuated by \(10^{-6}\), while an error in reading two is amplified by \(10^6\). Their product of length factors is one, so area preservation permits severe anisotropy.

Predict the determinant after redefining actuator units

Let \(x=S\widetilde x\) for \(S=\operatorname{diag}(2,3,4)\) in (4.3). How does the determinant change? Compare with changing input and output coordinates by the same \(S\).

Solution. With unchanged outputs the new matrix is \(A_\epsilon S\) and its determinant is \(24\epsilon\). If the output is also expressed as \(b=S\widetilde b\), the matrix becomes \(S^{-1}A_\epsilon S\), and its determinant is \(\epsilon\). The first action changes only the input volume unit. The second supplies compensating input and output scale factors.

Construct a determinant-preserving measurement edit

Suppose the sensor software adds three times channel one to channel three after measuring (4.3). What determinant and compatibility information remain?

Solution. This operation adds three times row one to row three, so the determinant remains \(\epsilon\). At \(\epsilon=0\) the old compatibility relation was \(b_3=b_1+b_2\). The new third reading is \(\widetilde b_3=b_3+3b_1\), so the new relation is \(\widetilde b_3=4b_1+b_2\). At nonzero \(\epsilon\) the new system is invertible because the channel edit is itself invertible.

Sources and limits

Lecture 11, determinants and Lecture 12, determinant properties supply the course topics. The signed-permutation derivation and sensor extension develop those topics independently. Cofactor expansion exposes algebraic structure. For a dense matrix the direct signed sum uses \(n!\) terms, whereas elimination uses a number of arithmetic operations proportional to \(n^3\). A signed logarithm of the determinant is useful when an explicit determinant product falls outside the floating-point range. A determinant threshold requires attention to the scaling of the entire matrix.
