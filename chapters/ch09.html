Chapter 9 · Lectures 30–34

Characteristic polynomials, similarity, and diagonalization

L30 L31 L32 L33 L34

A sampled state estimator updates two error components at each step. The second component decays on its own, while some of it reappears in the first component. A team planning when to stop taking measurements needs the error after many updates. Two scalar decay factors can simplify that prediction if their directions span the state space. A repeated decay factor may leave the coupling intact.

Use the original model \(z_{k+1}=Az_k\) for steps \(k=0,1,\ldots\), where \(z_k\in\mathbb C^2\) and \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\0&d\end{bmatrix}\) is fixed. The entries \(a,d\) are per-step decay factors and \(c\) is a transfer coefficient. In the running numerical case, \((a,c,d)=(0.8,0.3,0.5)\). Working over \(\mathbb C\) allows complex roots for real as well as complex update matrices.

Directions preserved by the update (L30 14:03)

For a square matrix \(A\) of order \(n\), a scalar \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue if \(Av=\lambda v\) for some nonzero vector \(v\), called a right eigenvector. The eigenspace for \(\lambda\) is \(\ker(A-\lambda I_n)\), with \(I_n\) the identity. It contains the zero vector and all eigenvectors of that eigenvalue. One update multiplies \(v\) by \(\lambda\), and induction gives \(A^kv=\lambda^kv\). In the two-state model, the first coordinate vector \(e_1=(1,0)^T\) has eigenvalue \(a\). For \(d\ne a\), the vector \(v_d=(c/(d-a),1)^T\) satisfies the eigenvector equation. Its first component accounts for the transfer from the second component.

The eigenspace records which initial conditions evolve through a single scalar. With \(z_0=(0,1)^T\) in the numerical case, the initial state is \(e_1+v_d\) and contains two such components. Their first coordinates start by cancelling. After one update the scalar factors differ, leaving a nonzero first coordinate.

Roots, multiplicities, and independent directions (L31 10:32, L32 00:29)

Use the monic characteristic polynomial \(p_A(t)=\det(tI_n-A)\), with \(t\) an indeterminate. The determinant criterion for invertibility gives \(p_A(\lambda)=0\) exactly when \(A-\lambda I_n\) has a nonzero null vector. For the two-state matrix, triangularity gives \(p_A(t)=(t-a)(t-d)\) independently of \(c\). Its roots encode both diagonal decay factors and leave \(c\) undetermined.

The coefficient of \(t^{n-1}\) is \(-\operatorname{tr}A\), the negative sum of diagonal entries. In the determinant expansion, a contribution of this degree must use \(n-1\) diagonal factors \(t\) and one factor \(-a_{ii}\). A nonidentity permutation moves at least two indices and has degree at most \(n-2\). Evaluating at \(t=0\) gives \(p_A(0)=(-1)^n\det A\). Factoring the polynomial over \(\mathbb C\) therefore gives the sum and product of eigenvalues with multiplicity as \(\operatorname{tr}A\) and \(\det A\). A real \(A\) has real polynomial coefficients, so a nonreal root and its conjugate have the same multiplicity.

The algebraic multiplicity of \(\lambda\) is its root multiplicity in \(p_A\). The geometric multiplicity is the dimension \(g\) of \(\ker(A-\lambda I_n)\). Pick a basis of this eigenspace and complete it to a basis of \(\mathbb C^n\). In those coordinates the update has diagonal blocks \(\lambda I_g\) and an \((n-g)\times(n-g)\) matrix, and a zero block below \(\lambda I_g\). Taking the determinant in the new basis factors out \((t-\lambda)^g\). Thus geometric multiplicity is at most algebraic multiplicity. A repeated root supplies as many independent eigenvectors as the dimension of its eigenspace.

Eigenvectors from distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. For a proof, assume a minimal nontrivial dependence \(\sum_i\alpha_i v_i=0\) among eigenvectors with distinct eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\). Apply \(A-\lambda_j I_n\) for one participating index \(j\). The \(j\)th term vanishes. Every other coefficient \(\alpha_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)\) remains nonzero, contradicting minimality. The same argument, applied to one sum of vectors from each eigenspace, shows that distinct eigenspaces have a direct sum. Consequently, combining a basis of each eigenspace gives a basis of the full space precisely when the geometric multiplicities sum to \(n\).

Similarity is a coordinate change (L33 14:31)

The columns of an invertible matrix \(S\) describe a basis in old coordinates. If \(z=Sy\) represents the same state using new coordinates \(y\), then \(Az=ASy\) has new coordinates \(S^{-1}ASy\). The matrix \(S^{-1}AS\) is similar to \(A\). Two similar matrices may give different entries for the same linear map in their two bases.

Similarity preserves the characteristic polynomial. Multiplying out \(tI_n-S^{-1}AS=S^{-1}(tI_n-A)S\) and using multiplicativity cancels \(\det S\). Similarity also preserves geometric multiplicities. The map \(y\mapsto Sy\) is a bijection from \(\ker(S^{-1}AS-\lambda I_n)\) to \(\ker(A-\lambda I_n)\). Equality of characteristic polynomials does not guarantee similarity. When \(a=d\) and \(c\ne0\), \(A-aI_2\) has rank one. For \(aI_2\), its shifted rank is zero. Both have characteristic polynomial \((t-a)^2\).

Diagonal coordinates and coupled limits (L34 00:31)

A matrix is diagonalizable over a field if it is similar over that field to a diagonal matrix. If the columns of \(S\) are independent eigenvectors, \(AS=SD\) for \(D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)\), where column \(i\) has eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\). Conversely, \(AS=SD\) with \(S\) invertible supplies \(n\) independent eigenvectors by reading the columns. A matrix with \(n\) distinct roots over its field is diagonalizable by the independence result. A real rotation through a quarter turn has roots \(\pm i\), so that argument yields diagonalizability over \(\mathbb C\) only.

For \(a\ne d\), the state model admits \(S=[e_1\ \ v_d]\), and \(A^k=SD^kS^{-1}\) follows by cancelling adjacent \(S^{-1}S\) factors. In the numerical case \(v_d=(-1,1)^T\) and \(A^kz_0=(0.8^k-0.5^k,0.5^k)^T\). The transfer term for arbitrary \(a\ne d\) follows either from this diagonalization or the update recurrence:

\[(A^k)_{12} =c\,\frac{a^k-d^k}{a-d} =c\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a^{k-1-j}d^j,\qquad k\geq1.\]

For fixed \(c\ne0\), as \(d\) approaches \(a\), the two eigendirections approach the same line. The quotient appears to divide by a vanishing number, but for \(k\geq 1\) the finite sum has limit \(cka^{k-1}\), with value \(c\) at \(k=1\). At \(d=a\) and \(c\ne0\), the eigenspace requires the second coordinate to vanish, so it has dimension one. The power identity survives the repeated-root limit. An eigenbasis does not.

Computing roots of a formed characteristic polynomial is convenient for symbolic examples and can introduce numerical sensitivity. For two nearly equal factors, even the displayed quotient can subtract nearly equal powers. A numerical workflow can retain the triangular coupling directly. The LAPACK guide describes how its general eigenproblem routines reduce to triangular or quasi-triangular Schur form. LAPACK Users’ Guide, Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors and Schur Factorization

Laboratory

Run ch09.py with Python and NumPy. It takes two fixed update matrices, reconstructs the distinct-root prediction from the eigenbasis, and checks the repeated-root formula against direct matrix powers. It prints the states after ten steps and has no persistent effects. The observed distinct-root state was \((0.10639762,0.00097656)^T\). Replacing \(d\) with \(0.8\) gave \((0.40265318,0.10737418)^T\) at the same step. The second experiment changes the decay factor as well as the multiplicity. It illustrates the formula in the coupled case, rather than isolating multiplicity as a causal variable.

Exercises and solutions

  1. Find the eigenspaces and trace of the original numerical update.
    Solution. At \(\lambda=0.8\), subtracting \(0.8 I_2\) forces the second coordinate to vanish, giving \(\operatorname{span}\{(1,0)^T\}\). At \(0.5\), the first row of \(A-0.5 I_2\) gives \(0.3(v_1+v_2)=0\), giving \(\operatorname{span}\{(-1,1)^T\}\). The trace \(1.3\) equals the sum of the two eigenvalues.

  2. Determine when \(\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\0&a\end{bmatrix}\) is diagonalizable.
    Solution. Its only eigenvalue is \(a\). For \(c\ne0\), the shifted matrix sends \((v_1,v_2)^T\) to \((cv_2,0)^T\), so it has a one-dimensional null space and there is no eigenbasis. For \(c=0\), the matrix is \(aI_2\) and every basis is an eigenbasis.

  3. For the original update, choose \(z_0=(s,1)^T\) to eliminate the \(0.8^k\) term.
    Solution. Write \(z_0=(s+1)e_1+v_d\). The first coefficient vanishes when \(s=-1\), placing the initial state in the eigenspace of \(0.5\). If \(s\) is misestimated by \(\epsilon\), the unwanted contribution to the first coordinate is \(\epsilon\,0.8^k\).

  4. Show that every diagonalizable matrix with only the eigenvalue \(\lambda\) equals \(\lambda I_n\).
    Solution. Its diagonal representation has all diagonal entries equal to \(\lambda\), so \(A=S(\lambda I_n)S^{-1}=\lambda I_n\). A matrix with the same characteristic polynomial may have nonzero coupling, as the repeated-root case demonstrates.

The number of roots controls the number of candidate scalar updates. The dimensions of the eigenspaces determine whether arbitrary initial errors decompose into independently evolving pieces.
