# Inner products and orthogonal coordinates

The instrument in [Chapter 2](ch02.html) can produce any reading $(s,t,s+t)^T$. Suppose its measured reading is $b=(2,1,4)^T$, and the measurement must be replaced by a physically compatible estimate. The correction depends on how much error each sensor can tolerate. The third channel may be four times as heavily weighted in squared error as either of the others. An inner product makes the weighting explicit, while an orthonormal basis converts the closest-point calculation into coordinate extraction.

This chapter develops the topics in Lectures 8–10. The instrument calculation, weighted fit, and floating-point comparison are original examples or extensions.

## Inner products specify geometry

[L8 09:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CLE8NpJOGOI&t=572s)

On a real or complex vector space $V$, an inner product $\langle u,v\rangle$ is a scalar-valued pairing that is linear in $v$, obeys $\langle v,u\rangle=\overline{\langle u,v\rangle}$, and gives a positive real value for $\langle v,v\rangle$ for every $v\ne0$. The bar denotes complex conjugation. These conditions give conjugate linearity in $u$. In coordinates the standard example is $u^*v$, with $u^*$ the conjugate transpose. The associated length is $\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}$. Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is zero.

The course uses the convention that the inner product is linear in the first argument, writing the standard inner product of $x$ and $y$ as $y^*x$. Here the arguments appear in the reverse order. The underlying scalar for a projection coefficient is the same in either convention when used consistently. In matrix multiplication $(AB)_{ij}$ is the coordinate inner product of the conjugated transpose of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$, giving one entry of the composed map. [L8 00:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CLE8NpJOGOI&t=17s)

For $v\ne0$, set $c=\langle v,u\rangle/\langle v,v\rangle$. Then $u-cv$ is orthogonal to $v$, and expanding the squared length gives

$$
0\leq\|u-cv\|^2=\|u\|^2-\frac{|\langle v,u\rangle|^2}{\|v\|^2}.
\tag{3.1}
$$

Thus $|\langle v,u\rangle|\leq\|v\|\|u\|$, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Equality requires $u-cv=0$. For $v=0$ equality also holds. The triangle inequality follows by writing $\|u+v\|^2=\|u\|^2+\|v\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\langle u,v\rangle$ and applying the bound to the last term. If $u$ and $v$ are orthogonal, the cross term is zero, which is the Pythagorean identity. In complex space a zero real part of the pairing suffices for this identity for a single pair. Orthogonality requires the entire pairing to vanish.

In the sensor model take $\langle u,v\rangle_W=u^*Wv$ with $W=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,4)$. More generally, $W$ defines an inner product exactly when it is Hermitian positive definite, meaning $W^*=W$ and $u^*Wu>0$ for nonzero $u$. For necessity, conjugate symmetry implies $u^*(W-W^*)v=0$ for all $u,v$. Testing coordinate vectors makes every entry of $W-W^*$ vanish, and positivity gives the second condition. For sufficiency, these two conditions provide conjugate symmetry and positivity, and matrix multiplication gives linearity in the second argument. The weights set a concrete metric. A 0.1-unit discrepancy in the third channel contributes $4(0.1)^2$ to squared length, matching a 0.2-unit discrepancy in the first channel. The model assumes a diagonal weight, so the squared cost adds channel by channel. The choice of weights is part of the inference problem.

## Orthonormal bases and least squares

[L9 00:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K_AOn_zwM-o&t=48s)

A family $q_1,\ldots,q_k$ is orthonormal if each vector has length one and any two distinct vectors are orthogonal. If $S$ is their span, define

$$
P_S b=\sum_{i=1}^k q_i\langle q_i,b\rangle.
\tag{3.2}
$$

For each $q_j$, the inner product with $b-P_Sb$ is zero because the only nonzero term in the sum occurs at $i=j$. Thus $b-P_Sb$ lies in $S^\perp$, and $\|b-z\|^2=\|b-P_Sb\|^2+\|P_Sb-z\|^2$ for any $z\in S$. This proves existence and uniqueness of the closest compatible output once an orthonormal basis is available. The squared distance grows by the squared extra displacement in $S$.

In the instrument, let $S=\operatorname{span}\{a_1,a_2\}$ with $a_1=(1,0,1)^T$, $a_2=(0,1,1)^T$, and let $C=[a_1\ a_2]$. This is the range from Chapter 2. Ordinary orthogonality gives $S^\perp=\operatorname{span}\{y\}$ for $y=(-1,-1,1)^T$. Since $y^*b=1$ and $y^*y=3$, the ordinary projection is $b-y/3=(7/3,4/3,11/3)^T$. Under $W$, the complementary direction is $W^{-1}y=(-1,-1,1/4)^T$ because $\langle W^{-1}y,z\rangle_W=y^*z$. The coefficient of the residual along this vector must enforce $y^*P_Sb=0$. Specifically, $y^*W^{-1}y=9/4$, so the weighted projection equals

$$
b-\frac{y^*b}{y^*W^{-1}y}W^{-1}y
=\left(\frac{22}{9},\frac{13}{9},\frac{35}{9}\right)^T.
\tag{3.3}
$$

The weighted third-channel correction has magnitude $1/9$, compared with $1/3$ in ordinary geometry. Increased penalty moved correction into channels one and two. The fitted output is unique, while any actuators satisfying $Ax=P_Sb$ still differ by vectors in the nullspace of the full calibration matrix $A$.

## Gram–Schmidt yields a triangular factor

[L9 04:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K_AOn_zwM-o&t=242s)

Given independent vectors $a_1,\ldots,a_k$, build $q_j$ by removing the projection onto the already processed span and normalizing the remainder. With indices $1\leq i<j\leq k$, write

$$
r_{ij}=\langle q_i,a_j\rangle,\qquad
w_j=a_j-\sum_{i<j}q_i r_{ij},\qquad
r_{jj}=\|w_j\|,\qquad q_j=w_j/r_{jj}.
\tag{3.4}
$$

Equation (3.2) makes $w_j$ orthogonal to the preceding $q_i$. It is nonzero because a zero remainder would put $a_j$ in the preceding span. Each step preserves the span of the first $j$ processed vectors, since $q_j$ lies in the new span and (3.4) expresses $a_j$ in terms of $q_1,\ldots,q_j$. If the family is dependent, a zero remainder records a redundant vector. Skipping it and retaining the earlier basis still constructs an orthonormal basis for the eventual span.

For the weighted instrument, $r_{11}=\sqrt5$ and $q_1=a_1/\sqrt5$. Also $r_{12}=4/\sqrt5$ and $w_2=a_2-\tfrac45a_1=(-4/5,1,1/5)^T$. Then $w_2^*Ww_2=9/5$ and $q_2=\sqrt5\,w_2/3$. Orthogonality follows from $a_1^*Ww_2=-4/5+4(1/5)=0$. Form $Q=[q_1\ q_2]$ and put the $r_{ij}$ in a matrix $R$, with zeros below its diagonal. Rearranging (3.4) column by column gives $C=QR$. The weighted coordinate identities are

$$
Q^*WQ=I_2,\qquad P_S=QQ^*W,\qquad
Rx=Q^*Wb.
\tag{3.5}
$$

Here $I_2$ is the two-dimensional identity and $x$ contains the coefficients of the fitted output $Cx$. The last equality follows by inserting $C=QR$ into $Cx=P_Sb$ and multiplying by $Q^*W$. Positive diagonal entries make $R$ invertible, and an upper triangular system can be solved from the bottom row upward. The argument works for any full column rank $C$. For a rank-deficient model the projected output remains unique by (3.2), while coefficients in the original column family can be nonunique.

Classical Gram–Schmidt computes each projection coefficient from the original $a_j$. Modified Gram–Schmidt instead computes it from the remainder after earlier subtractions. In exact arithmetic $q_i$ is orthogonal to the earlier $q_\ell$, so both compute the same coefficient. Floating-point roundoff can leave components in directions that were meant to have been removed. Normalizing a tiny remainder amplifies such errors. In addition to reconstruction, numerical checks should test the near-orthonormality of $Q$. The two algorithms and their differing finite-precision behavior are analyzed in [Giraud, Langou, and Rozložník, section 2](https://disciplinas.ime.unicamp.br/ms993/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2023/08/2005-CMA-giraud-langou-rozloznik.pdf).

## Orthogonal and unitary matrices

[L10 01:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J-BCvUALQyQ&t=66s)

In standard coordinates, a real square matrix $U$ is orthogonal if $U^TU=I$. A complex square matrix is unitary if $U^*U=I$. Those equations say precisely that the columns are orthonormal. The equation $U^*U=I$ gives injectivity and therefore, for a square matrix, invertibility. Consequently $U^{-1}=U^*$ and $UU^*=I$, so its rows also form an orthonormal family. The equality $\langle Uu,Uv\rangle=u^*U^*Uv=u^*v$ shows that such changes of coordinates preserve all pairings.

A rectangular matrix with orthonormal columns still preserves inner products on its input space, but its range may be proper. In ordinary geometry $Q^*Q=I_k$ and $QQ^*$ is the orthogonal projector onto $\mathcal R(Q)$, where $k$ is the number of columns. For example, taking $Q$ to consist of the first two columns of $I_3$ gives $QQ^*=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0)$. Under a weight $W$, the counterpart is $QQ^*W$. This matrix squares to itself because $Q^*WQ=I_k$. Orthogonality of its residual is in the weighted geometry. A weighted projector can fail to equal its ordinary conjugate transpose.

[Run or inspect the Python laboratory](../code/ch03.py). It accepts no inputs, uses NumPy, prints results, and raises an exception if the checks fail. It compares the weighted projection from a hand-derived residual with the one computed by Gram–Schmidt, then checks a complex factorization using conjugate inner products. The final experiment uses four columns, each with first entry one and a distinct near-zero entry $10^{-8}$ beneath it. It reports both reconstruction and the Frobenius size of the orthogonality defect, where the Frobenius size is the square root of the sum of squared entry magnitudes. This deliberately sensitive example is a diagnostic of the tested implementations and arithmetic.

In the recorded run, the reconstruction errors were below $10^{-24}$ for both variants, while the orthogonality defects were approximately $1.225$ for classical Gram–Schmidt and $1.225\times10^{-8}$ for modified Gram–Schmidt. Small reconstruction error alone would have missed the large loss of orthogonality.

## Exercises with worked solutions

### Fix the complex projection coefficient

Use the standard complex inner product to project $u=(1,0)^T$ onto the span of $v=(i,1)^T$, where $i^2=-1$. Test the residual.

**Solution.** Since $v^*u=-i$ and $v^*v=2$, the projection is $(-i/2)v=(1/2,-i/2)^T$. The residual is $(1/2,i/2)^T$, and its pairing with $v$ is $-i/2+i/2=0$. Conjugating the numerator would reverse the coefficient and destroy orthogonality.

### Reweight the third sensor

Replace $W$ by $\operatorname{diag}(1,1,\omega)$, where $\omega>0$. Obtain the third-channel estimate for $b=(2,1,4)^T$ and its limit as $\omega$ grows.

**Solution.** The residual is $W^{-1}y/(2+1/\omega)$ because $y^*b=1$. Its third component is $1/(2\omega+1)$. The estimate is $4-1/(2\omega+1)$ in that channel, approaching four as the penalty grows. The first two residual components tend to $-1/2$ each so that the limiting readings remain compatible.

### Process a redundant calibration column

Apply Gram–Schmidt to $a_1,a_2,a_3$ with $a_3=2a_1-a_2$. Find the third remainder and explain its meaning.

**Solution.** The first two orthonormal vectors span both $a_1$ and $a_2$, hence also $a_3$. Equation (3.2) applied to $a_3$ returns $a_3$ itself, so $w_3=0$. The zero remainder certifies redundancy in exact arithmetic. $a_3$ introduces no new output direction.

### Detect a weighted projector

Let $Q=(1,1)^T/\sqrt5$ and $W=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)$. Calculate $P=QQ^*W$ and verify idempotence and weighted residual orthogonality on $b=(0,1)^T$.

**Solution.** $P=\tfrac15\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\1&4\end{pmatrix}$. Multiplying the unscaled matrix by itself gives five times itself, so $P^2=P$. The residual is $(-4/5,1/5)^T$ and pairing with $Q$ in the weighted geometry gives $(-4/5+4/5)/\sqrt5=0$. The entries $P_{12}=4/5$ and $P_{21}=1/5$ differ.

### Check a complex isometry

Show that $U=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}$ is unitary. Identify what goes wrong if conjugation is omitted.

**Solution.** The two columns have squared length one, and their pairing is $(i-i)/2=0$. Thus $U^*U=I_2$. The plain transpose would give diagonal entries $(1+i^2)/2=0$ and off-diagonal entries $i$. Ordinary transposition does not represent complex coordinate orthogonality.
