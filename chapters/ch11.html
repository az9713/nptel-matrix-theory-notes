Unitary geometry and Schur form

L37 L38 L39 L40

A controller team rotates its sensor axes and sees the off-diagonal entries of its update matrix change. It needs to tell which changes arise from the coordinates and how much error still transfers between states. In this original dimensionless model, an orthonormal change of axes exposes the transfer without stretching the measurement geometry.

Let \(A=\begin{bmatrix}0.8&3\\0&0.5\end{bmatrix}\) update a state \(x\in\mathbb C^2\). The input \(e_2=(0,1)^T\) has Euclidean norm one. The output \(Ae_2=(3,0.5)^T\) has norm \(\sqrt{9.25}\) even though both eigenvalues have magnitude below one. Following that input through a change of basis distinguishes a real one-step amplification from its coordinate representation.

Unitary matrices and length preservation (L37 04:01, L38 00:50)

Use the standard inner product \(x^*y\) on \(\mathbb C^n\), with \(x^*\) the conjugate transpose, and use \(\|x\|_2=\sqrt{x^*x}\). A square matrix \(Q\) of order \(n\) is unitary if \(Q^*Q=I_n\). This condition states that the columns have inner products given by the entries of \(I_n\). Such columns form an orthonormal basis, since \(Q^*Q=I_n\) makes \(Q\) injective and hence invertible in equal finite dimensions. Therefore \(Q^{-1}=Q^*\) and \(QQ^*=I_n\), which also makes the rows orthonormal. A rectangular matrix with orthonormal columns preserves input lengths, but its product with its adjoint projects onto its range.

The identity \((Qx)^*Qy=x^*y\) establishes inner-product preservation. Conversely, if a square linear map preserves lengths for every vector, then \(H=Q^*Q-I_n\) satisfies \(H=H^*\) and \(x^*Hx=0\) for every \(x\). Testing standard coordinate vectors \(e_j\) gives zero diagonal entries. Testing \(e_j+e_k\) and \(e_j+i e_k\) gives zero real and imaginary parts of each off-diagonal entry. Thus \(Q\) is unitary. The real case yields an orthogonal matrix, satisfying \(Q^TQ=I_n\). Taking determinants gives \(|\det Q|=1\). If \(Qv=\lambda v\) for nonzero \(v\), equal lengths imply \(|\lambda|=1\). A product of unitary matrices is unitary because it composes inner-product-preserving maps.

Rotate the estimator axes using \(Q=\frac15\begin{bmatrix}3&-4\\4&3\end{bmatrix}\). Its columns have squared lengths \((9+16)/25=1\) and mutual dot product zero. Define the rotated operator \(M=QAQ^*\) and mapped input \(Qe_2\). Then \(M(Qe_2)=QAe_2\), retaining norm \(\sqrt{9.25}\). The induced matrix norm \(\|A\|_2=\sup_{\|x\|_2=1}\|Ax\|_2\) also survives rotation because \(Q\) maps the unit sphere bijectively to itself.

One basis or two? (L39 09:15)

Unitary similarity uses the same orthonormal coordinate system for domain and codomain. Its form is \(Q^*AQ\). It preserves the characteristic polynomial by the ordinary similarity identity, and \((Q^*AQ)^k=Q^*A^kQ\) captures repeated updates in the new coordinates. Unitary equivalence also denotes this relation in operator terminology. For independent input and output bases, use the explicit term two-sided unitary equivalence.

Two-sided unitary equivalence writes \(C=U^*AV\) for an \(m\times n\) matrix \(A\) and independent square unitaries \(U,V\) of respective orders \(m,n\). It suits a map with different measurement axes at input and output. In particular \(C^*C=V^*A^*AV\), so \(C^*C\) and \(A^*A\) have the same characteristic polynomial. Also \(\|Cy\|_2=\|AVy\|_2\) for every \(y\). The eigenvalues of a square \(A\) need not survive. In the estimator, take \(U=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)\), \(V=I_2\). Reversing the first output sign preserves \(C^*C=A^*A\), but changes the triangular diagonal and thus the spectrum to \(-0.8,0.5\). A state recurrence uses each output as the next input, so independent coordinate choices require a conversion between them to interpret repeated updates.

Schur triangularization (L40 09:30)

For every square complex \(M\) of order \(n\), there exists a unitary \(Z\) and upper triangular \(T\) with \(M=ZTZ^*\). Take a root \(\lambda\) of the characteristic polynomial and select a corresponding eigenvector \(z_1\) of Euclidean norm one. Complete it to an orthonormal basis with basis matrix \(Q_1\). The first column of \(Q_1^*MQ_1\) is \(\lambda e_1\). Its lower-right block \(M_2\) has order \(n-1\). Inductively triangularize \(M_2\) using a unitary \(Z_2\), then conjugate the whole matrix by \(\operatorname{diag}(1,Z_2)\). This preserves the zero block below the leading entry and makes the remaining diagonal block triangular. Order one is the starting case. The product \(Z=Q_1\operatorname{diag}(1,Z_2)\) is unitary.

The eigenvalues appear on the diagonal of \(T\). The first \(k\) columns of \(Z\) span an invariant subspace, a subspace mapped into itself by \(M\). The \(j\)th column of \(MZ=ZT\) is a combination of the first \(j\) columns of \(Z\) because \(T_{ij}=0\) for \(i>j\). In the original \(A\), the first coordinate direction is an eigenvector, and the second Schur vector is \(e_2\). Applying \(A\) to it produces \(3e_1+0.5e_2\), so the second Schur vector is not an eigenvector. At each inductive step a given remaining eigenvalue may be selected first, since the reduced characteristic polynomial factors out only the selected eigenvalue with one copy.

The real quarter-turn \(\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\) has roots \(\pm i\). Its Schur form in real arithmetic requires a \(2\times2\) diagonal block because a real triangular form would only have real eigenvalues. LAPACK computes Schur form through reduction to upper Hessenberg form, whose entries below the first subdiagonal vanish, followed by iteration to triangular form in the complex case or quasi-triangular form in the real case. The proof establishes existence and invariant-subspace structure. The numerical reduction operates without requiring a complete eigenbasis. How much coupling does Schur form retain?

The squared Frobenius norm \(\|M\|_F^2=\operatorname{tr}(M^*M)\) sums the squared magnitudes of all entries. The cyclic trace identity \(\operatorname{tr}(XY)=\operatorname{tr}(YX)\) follows by interchanging the summation indices in \(\sum_{ij}X_{ij}Y_{ji}\) for compatible products. It implies \(\|Z^*MZ\|_F=\|M\|_F\). Every complex Schur form has the same eigenvalue multiset on the diagonal, so

\[\sum_{i<j}|T_{ij}|^2=\|M\|_F^2-\sum_i|\lambda_i|^2.\]

In the two-state model, the right side is nine. Every Schur representation retains an off-diagonal coefficient of magnitude three, although its phase and the diagonal order can change. For more states the formula constrains the total squared magnitude and leaves its distribution among pairs open. Amplification over several steps depends on the detailed multiplication of the triangular form.

Exercises and solutions

  1. Show that a unitary lower triangular matrix is diagonal.
    Solution. Its inverse is lower triangular by forward substitution. Unitarity makes the inverse equal to its adjoint, which is upper triangular. Thus the inverse is diagonal, and the original matrix is diagonal with diagonal entries of modulus one.

  2. Determine whether \(A\) and \(\operatorname{diag}(0.8,0.5)\) can be unitarily similar.
    Solution. The eigenvalues agree. The total squared Schur off-diagonal magnitudes are nine for \(A\) and zero for the diagonal model. Unitary similarity preserves this total, ruling out the relation.

  3. Give the matrix of the restriction of \(M\) to its first \(k\) Schur directions.
    Solution. Let \(Z_k\) contain those columns. Upper triangularity gives \(MZ_k=Z_kT_{1:k,1:k}\). Since \(Z_k^*Z_k=I_k\), multiplication on the left by \(Z_k^*\) gives the restriction in this orthonormal basis.

  4. Find an orthonormal eigenbasis over \(\mathbb C\) for the real quarter-turn.
    Solution. Direct multiplication gives \((1,-i)^T\) and \((1,i)^T\) for eigenvalues \(i\) and \(-i\). Their inner product is \(1+i^2=0\) and both norms are \(\sqrt2\). Dividing by \(\sqrt2\) gives the basis. A real eigenbasis is impossible because both eigenvalues are nonreal.

Schur coordinates provide invariant subspaces without distorting Euclidean geometry. The entries above the diagonal preserve information about transfer that a list of eigenvalues leaves out.
