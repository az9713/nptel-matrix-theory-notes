Dual norms and matrix amplification

L17 L18 L19 L20 L21 L22

A calibration operator turns actuator error into sensor error. Two audit questions require different optimizations. How large can a particular sensor discrepancy become under a prescribed error budget? How large can the entire reading error become? A dual norm answers the first question for a scalar measurement. An induced matrix norm answers the second for a vector output.

Dual norms quantify the largest scalar response

L17 01:09 · L18 05:05

For a norm \(N\) on \(\mathbb F^n\), with \(\mathbb F=\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\), write its dual as

\[N^\circ(y)=\max_{N(x)\leq1}|y^*x|. \tag{6.1}\]

The unit ball is compact by finite-dimensional norm equivalence, so the continuous scalar objective attains a maximum. If \(y\ne0\), the maximum is positive because \(x=y/N(y)\) is feasible with \(y^*x>0\). Scaling shows that some maximizer lies on the unit sphere. Absolute homogeneity holds because multiplying \(y\) by a scalar scales every objective by its magnitude. Applying the triangle inequality inside the maximum gives \(N^\circ(y+z)\leq N^\circ(y)+N^\circ(z)\). Thus (6.1) defines a norm. The absolute value can be replaced by the real part in the maximum because rotating \(x\) by a scalar of modulus one preserves feasibility and can make \(y^*x\) nonnegative real.

For general \(x\), normalization by \(N(x)\) gives \(|y^*x|\leq N^\circ(y)N(x)\), also valid at zero. In the other direction the definition of duality gives \((N^\circ)^\circ(x)\leq N(x)\). Equality follows from a supporting hyperplane to the closed convex unit ball at \(x/N(x)\) for \(x\ne0\). In real coordinates its functional is \(\operatorname{Re}(y^*z)\) for some nonzero \(y\). Its maximum over the ball is \(N^\circ(y)\) by phase rotation, and it attains this value at \(x/N(x)\). Normalize \(y\) so \(N^\circ(y)=1\) to obtain \(y^*x\) of magnitude \(N(x)\). This uses the finite-dimensional supporting-hyperplane theorem. It says that a unit scalar test can attain the length bound at every nonzero vector, although the test generally depends on the point.

For \(1<p<\infty\), Hölder gives \((\|\cdot\|_p)^\circ(y)\leq\|y\|_q\), with \(1/p+1/q=1\). For \(y\ne0\) choose

\[x_j=\frac{y_j}{|y_j|}\left(\frac{|y_j|}{\|y\|_q}\right)^{q-1} \quad(y_j\ne0), \tag{6.2}\]

and set \(x_j=0\) at zeros of \(y\). Since \((q-1)p=q\), this vector has \(p\)-length one. The scalar response is \(\|y\|_q\), proving equality. At \(p=1\), a coordinate attaining the largest \(|y_j|\) gives dual length \(\|y\|_\infty\). At \(p=\infty\), choosing the phase \(y_j/|y_j|\) in every nonzero coordinate gives dual length \(\|y\|_1\). These examples also explain the calibration correction in Chapter 5. For \(y\ne0\) under the scalar constraint \(y^*r=1\), the smallest \(N(r)\) is \(1/N^\circ(y)\), since the bound is attained by a rescaled, phase-aligned maximizer from (6.1).

Entrywise and product-sensitive matrix norms

L19 03:32 · L20 13:01

A matrix can be viewed as a vector of entries. Any vector norm on those entries is a norm on matrices as a vector space. On square matrices the course also requires submultiplicativity, \(M(AB)\leq M(A)M(B)\). This property bounds the effect of a product using bounds for its factors. The entrywise maximum \(M(A)=\max_{i,j}|a_{ij}|\) fails it for matrices of all ones of size \(n>1\), since the product has every entry \(n\) but each factor has maximum one. For products through an intermediate space of dimension \(k\), the direct upper bound is \(kM(A)M(B)\) by summing \(k\) entry products.

The Frobenius norm \(\|A\|_F=(\sum_{i,j}|a_{ij}|^2)^{1/2}\) is submultiplicative even for compatible rectangular products. By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[|(AB)_{ij}|^2 \leq \left(\sum_k|a_{ik}|^2\right)\left(\sum_k|b_{kj}|^2\right). \tag{6.3}\]

Summing over \(i,j\) factors the right side into \(\|A\|_F^2\|B\|_F^2\). There is also a coordinate-free interpretation for Euclidean spaces. If \(a_j\) are the columns of \(A\), then \(\|A\|_F^2=\sum_j\|a_j\|_2^2=\operatorname{tr}(A^*A)\), where the trace is the sum of diagonal entries. Left multiplication by a unitary preserves each column length, so the Frobenius norm stays the same. Taking the adjoint preserves the sum of squared entry magnitudes. Applying the left-multiplication argument to the adjoint therefore proves invariance under right multiplication by a unitary as well.

Induced norms are worst-case gains

L21 01:39 · L22 04:30

Given input and output norms \(N_{\mathrm{in}}\) and \(N_{\mathrm{out}}\), define

\[\|A\|_{\mathrm{in}\to\mathrm{out}} =\max_{N_{\mathrm{in}}(x)=1}N_{\mathrm{out}}(Ax). \tag{6.4}\]

The input sphere is compact, and continuity of \(N_{\mathrm{out}}(Ax)\) follows from norm equivalence and continuity of coordinate matrix multiplication. Normalization gives \(N_{\mathrm{out}}(Ax)\leq \|A\|_{\mathrm{in}\to\mathrm{out}}N_{\mathrm{in}}(x)\). The smallest constant in this bound is (6.4), because a unit maximizer attains it. If the constant is zero, every coordinate input maps to zero, hence \(A=0\). Absolute homogeneity and the triangle inequality follow by applying them inside the maximum. For a product \(AB\), apply the vector bound first to \(A\) and then to \(B\). When the output norm for \(B\) agrees with the input norm for \(A\), the product bound is \(\|AB\|_{\mathrm{in}\to\mathrm{out}}\leq\|A\|_{\mathrm{mid}\to\mathrm{out}}\|B\|_{\mathrm{in}\to\mathrm{mid}}\). An induced square norm uses the same norm in both spaces and gives \(\|I\|=1\). For \(n>1\) the Frobenius norm satisfies \(\|I_n\|_F=\sqrt n>1\), so the matrix norm need not itself arise from (6.4).

Use \(\|A\|_p\) to denote the norm induced by the coordinate \(p\)-norm on both spaces. The column-sum formula \(\|A\|_1=\max_j\sum_i|a_{ij}|\) follows by writing \(Ax=\sum_jx_j a_j\) and using the triangle inequality. A coordinate vector associated with a largest column sum attains the bound. For \(\|A\|_\infty\), each output magnitude is bounded by the absolute sum of its row times \(\|x\|_\infty\). For a row attaining the largest sum, choose \(x_j=\overline{a_{ij}}/|a_{ij}|\) where the row entry is nonzero and, for example, \(x_j=1\) at its zeros. All terms in the selected output add nonnegatively, so \(\|A\|_\infty=\max_i\sum_j|a_{ij}|\).

L22 10:03 · L22 16:04

Using the spectral theorem proved in Chapter 15, the matrix \(A^*A\) has an orthonormal eigenbasis and nonnegative eigenvalues. Expand any Euclidean unit vector in that basis. Its squared output length \(x^*A^*Ax\) is a weighted mean of the eigenvalues with weights equal to squared coordinate magnitudes. Its maximum is therefore the largest eigenvalue and is attained by a corresponding eigenvector. Thus \(\|A\|_2=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(A^*A)}\), the largest singular value. This norm is called the spectral norm. An operator tested in different coordinate budgets can have different worst-case input vectors.

For the reduced calibration map \(C=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\), each column sum is two and the largest row sum is two. Thus \(\|C\|_1=\|C\|_\infty=2\), attained at \((1,0)^T\) and \((1,1)^T\), respectively. But \(C^*C=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\) has eigenvalues three and one, so \(\|C\|_2=\sqrt3\), attained at \((1,1)^T/\sqrt2\). The Frobenius norm is two because it adds all four unit entries in quadrature. Even when the row- and column-sum numbers happen to agree, they measure gains with different admissible error shapes.

Exercises with worked solutions

Compute the dual of a weighted error budget

Let \(N(x)=\|Dx\|_\infty\) with \(D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n)\) and \(d_j>0\). Find \(N^\circ(y)\).

Solution. Substituting \(z=Dx\) gives \(y^*x=(D^{-1}y)^*z\) and the feasible set \(\|z\|_\infty\leq1\). The dual of the maximum norm is the sum norm, so \(N^\circ(y)=\sum_j|y_j|/d_j\). An attaining \(z\) aligns its phase with \(D^{-1}y\).

Check the norm of a rectangular rank-one map

Let \(A=uv^*\), with nonzero \(u\in\mathbb C^m\) and \(v\in\mathbb C^n\). Find its induced Euclidean norm and its Frobenius norm.

Solution. \(\|Ax\|_2=\|u\|_2|v^*x|\). By Cauchy–Schwarz this is bounded by \(\|u\|_2\|v\|_2\|x\|_2\), with equality at \(x=v/\|v\|_2\). The Frobenius sum is \(\sum_{i,j}|u_i|^2|v_j|^2=\|u\|_2^2\|v\|_2^2\), hence the two norms agree on this rank-one map.

Choose different input and output budgets

For the reduced calibration map \(C\), compute the induced gain from \(\ell_1\) inputs to \(\ell_\infty\) outputs. Here \(\ell_p\) denotes the coordinate norm \(\|\cdot\|_p\).

Solution. Every output is at most \(\max_{i,j}|c_{ij}|\sum_j|x_j|=1\) on the unit input sphere. Sending \((1,0)^T\) through \(C\) gives \((1,0,1)^T\), which attains the bound. The gain is one.

Compare Euclidean gain and column sum

Construct a \(2\times2\) matrix with induced one-norm larger than its induced Euclidean norm, and another with the opposite ordering.

Solution. Take \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\). Its one-norm is two and its Euclidean norm is \(\sqrt2\) by the rank-one computation. Taking \(A^T\) switches its nonzero column to a nonzero row. The new one-norm is one while the Euclidean norm remains \(\sqrt2\).

Separate product compatibility from scalar magnitude

On \(n\times n\) matrices let \(M(A)=\gamma\max_{i,j}|a_{ij}|\) with \(\gamma>0\). For what \(\gamma\) is it submultiplicative?

Solution. The intermediate dimension bound gives \(M(AB)\leq(n/\gamma)M(A)M(B)\), sufficient for \(\gamma\geq n\). The all-ones example yields \(\gamma n\leq\gamma^2\) as a necessary condition. Thus the smallest scale that makes this definition submultiplicative is \(n\).

Sources and limits

NPTEL Matrix Theory Lectures 17–22 establish the sequence from duality to induced norms. Murthy’s parallel IISc course index uses a separate session numbering for those topics. The sensor calculations and mixed-budget exercises are original. An induced norm is a worst-case bound over an entire vector sphere. If physically realizable errors occupy a smaller set, the attained gain on that set can be lower.
