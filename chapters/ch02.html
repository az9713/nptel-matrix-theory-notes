Fundamental subspaces and rank

L5 L6 L7

Four actuator settings produce three sensor readings in a calibration model. Two distinct command vectors can produce identical readings, and some target readings cannot be produced by any command. Those failures have different certificates. An invisible command is a nonzero vector mapped to zero. An impossible reading violates a relation that every output obeys.

This chapter develops the topics in Lectures 5–7. The calibration model and its calculations are original examples.

Four subspaces from one matrix

L5 00:39

Let \(\mathbb F\) denote \(\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\), let \(A\in\mathbb F^{m\times n}\), and write \(A^*\) for its conjugate transpose, which equals its transpose over \(\mathbb R\). Use \(\langle u,v\rangle=u^*v\) as the coordinate inner product. For a subspace \(S\), its orthogonal complement \(S^\perp\) consists of all vectors \(v\) in the same ambient space satisfying \(\langle u,v\rangle=0\) for every \(u\in S\).

The image \(\mathcal R(A)=\{Ax:x\in\mathbb F^n\}\) is the column space. If \(a_j\) is column \(j\), then \(Ax=\sum_j x_ja_j\). The kernel \(\ker A=\{x:Ax=0\}\) is the nullspace. Apply the same definitions to \(A^*\) to obtain \(\mathcal R(A^*)\), the adjoint column form of the row space, and \(\ker A^*\), the left nullspace. The complex convention sends each row to a conjugated column and puts that column in the input space. With this convention, each entry of \(Ax\) is the inner product of the corresponding conjugated row with \(x\). The ambient spaces matter.

Input space \(\mathbb F^n\) Output space \(\mathbb F^m\)
\(\mathcal R(A^*)\) contains observable input directions. \(\mathcal R(A)\) contains achievable outputs.
\(\ker A\) contains invisible input directions. \(\ker A^*\) contains linear tests that every achievable output passes.

For the calibration model take the real matrix

\[A=\begin{pmatrix} 1&0&1&2\\ 0&1&1&-1\\ 1&1&2&1 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{2.1}\]

All quantities in this model are dimensionless. The output of any command \(x=(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)^T\) has the form \((s,t,s+t)^T\), where \(s=x_1+x_3+2x_4\) and \(t=x_2+x_3-x_4\). The output determines these two combinations. For example, increasing \(x_3\) by one and decreasing both \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) by one leaves the reading unchanged. The target \((2,1,4)^T\) violates the required third reading \(2+1=3\). The vector \(y=(-1,-1,1)^T\) tests this discrepancy with \(y^*b=1\).

Bases and their dimensions

L7 02:02

The elementary row operations swap rows, scale one row by a nonzero scalar, or add a multiple of one row to another. They are invertible. If they turn \(A\) into \(R=EA\), then \(E\) is invertible and \(Rx=0\) if and only if \(Ax=0\). A row operation changes generators of the row space by invertible recombination, so it preserves \(\mathcal R(A^*)\). Correspondingly, \(\mathcal R(R)=E(\mathcal R(A))\), which may move the column space in the output coordinates. To obtain an image basis for the original matrix, reduce by choosing a leading nonzero entry in each successive row farther to the right. Scale each leading entry to one and eliminate the entries above and below it. In the resulting reduced row echelon form, the leading entries are called pivots. Their columns are distinct coordinate vectors in the nonzero rows, and every column is a combination of those vectors. Extract the corresponding columns from \(A\). Multiplying by \(E^{-1}\) preserves their independence and their span relation.

For (2.1), subtract both of the first two rows from row three. Columns one and two are the pivots. Thus \((1,0,1)^T\) and \((0,1,1)^T\) form the image basis. The nonzero reduced rows give the adjoint column basis \((1,0,1,2)^T\) and \((0,1,1,-1)^T\). For the kernel set \(s=t=0\). If \(x_3=u\) and \(x_4=v\), then \(x=(-u-2v,-u+v,u,v)^T\), which supplies the basis \((-1,-1,1,0)^T\) and \((-2,1,0,1)^T\). Finally, the left nullspace is the span of \((-1,-1,1)^T\) because orthogonality to both of the image basis vectors gives \(y_1=-y_3\) and \(y_2=-y_3\).

Define the rank \(r=\dim\mathcal R(A)\). The pivot proof gives \(r=\dim\mathcal R(A^*)\), since the nonzero reduced rows are independent and each supplies one pivot. There is also a coordinate-independent count for \(\dim\ker A\). Take a basis \(k_1,\ldots,k_\ell\) of the kernel and complete it to a basis of the input space by adding vectors outside the span of the growing list. Call these additional vectors \(w_1,\ldots,w_{n-\ell}\). The vectors \(Aw_i\) span the image because the \(k_i\) contribute zero. Any zero linear combination of the \(Aw_i\) would put the same combination of the \(w_i\) in the kernel. Uniqueness of basis coefficients then forces that combination to have all zero coefficients. Therefore the \(Aw_i\) form a basis of the image.

\[\dim\ker A=n-r,\qquad \dim\ker A^*=m-r. \tag{2.2}\]

The second identity applies the same basis argument to \(A^*\). For (2.1), \((m,n,r)=(3,4,2)\). The invisible input space has dimension two, and a single independent relation among the three outputs accounts for the one-dimensional left nullspace. The dimension and orthogonality parts of this result are developed with a geometric interpretation in Strang, “The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra,” pp. 848–850.

Compatibility and the observable representative

L6 00:13

For any input \(x\) and output test \(y\), the identity \(y^*Ax=(A^*y)^*x\) pairs the two spaces. If \(A^*y=0\), then \(y\) is orthogonal to \(Ax\) for every \(x\). Conversely, if \(y^*Ax=0\) for every \(x\), take \(x=A^*y\) to conclude \(A^*y=0\). Applying the same argument with \(A^*\) yields

\[(\mathcal R(A))^\perp=\ker A^*,\qquad (\mathcal R(A^*))^\perp=\ker A. \tag{2.3}\]

The two subspaces in each ambient space intersect only at zero because a vector orthogonal to itself is zero. Equation (2.2) gives dimensions adding to the ambient dimension, so each ambient space is the direct sum of its pair. In particular, \(Ax=b\) is solvable precisely when \(y^*b=0\) for every \(y\in\ker A^*\). Necessity follows by setting \(b=Ax\). For sufficiency decompose \(b=b_{\mathrm{im}}+b_{\mathrm{left}}\) along \(\mathcal R(A)\) and \(\ker A^*\). Taking \(y=b_{\mathrm{left}}\) in the assumed identities yields \(b_{\mathrm{left}}^*b_{\mathrm{left}}=0\). Every left null vector is an output test, and a basis of the left nullspace suffices to test compatibility.

For the compatible target \(b=(2,1,3)^T\), choose the particular solution \(x_0=(2,1,0,0)^T\). The full solution set is \(x_0+\ker A\) because differences of solutions satisfy the homogeneous equation. It has one member \(x_{\mathrm{obs}}\in\mathcal R(A^*)\), since decomposing \(x_0\) into its two orthogonal components removes a kernel vector without changing \(Ax_0\). In this case seek \(x_{\mathrm{obs}}=\alpha(1,0,1,2)^T+\beta(0,1,1,-1)^T\). The first two output equations give \(6\alpha-\beta=2\) and \(-\alpha+3\beta=1\), so \(\alpha=7/17\), \(\beta=8/17\), and \(x_{\mathrm{obs}}=(7,8,15,6)^T/17\). Its third reading is three. For \(k\in\ker A\) the squared coordinate length of \(x_{\mathrm{obs}}+k\) is \(x_{\mathrm{obs}}^*x_{\mathrm{obs}}+k^*k\). The cross terms vanish by (2.3), so \(x_{\mathrm{obs}}\) uniquely minimizes length among all exact commands. This length criterion assumes equally scaled, equally penalized coordinates.

Rank under composition and modification

L7 08:31

Let \(B:\mathbb F^p\to\mathbb F^n\) and retain \(A:\mathbb F^n\to\mathbb F^m\). The image of \(AB\) equals \(A(\mathcal R(B))\). Applying the kernel basis argument to the restriction of \(A\) to \(\mathcal R(B)\) gives the exact identity

\[\operatorname{rank}(AB)=\operatorname{rank}B-\dim(\mathcal R(B)\cap\ker A). \tag{2.4}\]

The dimension subtracted in (2.4) is how many independent output directions of \(B\) are invisible to \(A\). It is at most \(\dim\ker A\). Also \(A(\mathcal R(B))\subseteq\mathcal R(A)\). These two facts give

\[\operatorname{rank}A+\operatorname{rank}B-n \leq\operatorname{rank}(AB) \leq\min(\operatorname{rank}A,\operatorname{rank}B). \tag{2.5}\]

The lower bound is Sylvester's rank inequality. Its three-map extension is Frobenius's inequality \(\operatorname{rank}(ABC)+\operatorname{rank}B\geq\operatorname{rank}(AB)+\operatorname{rank}(BC)\), where the sizes allow both compositions. Apply (2.4) to \(A\) on \(\mathcal R(BC)\) and on \(\mathcal R(B)\). Since \(\mathcal R(BC)\subseteq\mathcal R(B)\), the first intersection with \(\ker A\) has dimension no larger than the second. Rearrangement gives the result. L7 14:32

In (2.1) choose \(B\) with columns \((1,0,0,0)^T\) and \((-1,-1,1,0)^T\). The columns are independent, but the second is invisible to \(A\). The image of \(AB\) is the line spanned by \((1,0,1)^T\), so the product has rank one. If both columns of \(B\) form the kernel basis of \(A\), the product rank drops to zero even though both maps have rank two.

For matrices \(A\) and \(\Delta\) of the same shape, \(\mathcal R(A+\Delta)\subseteq \mathcal R(A)+\mathcal R(\Delta)\), so \(\operatorname{rank}(A+\Delta)\leq\operatorname{rank}A+\operatorname{rank}\Delta\). Applying the same upper bound to \(A=(A+\Delta)-\Delta\) gives \(|\operatorname{rank}(A+\Delta)-\operatorname{rank}A|\leq\operatorname{rank}\Delta\). In (2.1), adding a nonzero \(\epsilon\) to the \((3,4)\) entry increases the rank to three. Columns one and two are unchanged, and the changed fourth column minus twice the first plus the second is \((0,0,\epsilon)^T\). This direction is outside the original image. Exact rank can change under arbitrarily small perturbations, so an exact missing direction and a weakly measured direction require different engineering decisions.

An \(m\times n\) matrix of rank \(r\) also has a rank factorization \(A=CF\). Here \(C\) is \(m\times r\) with independent columns chosen from \(A\), and \(F\) is \(r\times n\) with the coordinates of every original column in that basis. In (2.1), \(C\) contains columns one and two, and \(F\) contains the first two rows of \(A\). \(F\) computes the identifiable combinations \((s,t)\) and \(C\) maps those combinations to readings. If \(A=UV\) factors through \(k\) coordinates, (2.5) gives \(\operatorname{rank}A\leq k\), so \(r\) is the smallest intermediate dimension of a linear factorization.

Writing the product \(CF\) as a sum over its \(r\) intermediate coordinates expresses \(A\) as a sum of \(r\) outer products, each of one column of \(C\) and one row of \(F\). Each has rank one because every column is a multiple of a fixed nonzero column and at least one multiple is nonzero. Rank subadditivity proves that no sum with fewer rank-one terms represents \(A\). An invertible multiplication on either side preserves rank because the product rank cannot increase and multiplication by the inverse restores the original rank.

Laboratory

Run or inspect the Python laboratory. The script accepts no input and has no file or network effects. It uses NumPy to check the displayed bases, the solution equations, the compatible target's minimum-length command, and the product-rank example. A failed check raises an exception. The final comparison applies two user-visible rank thresholds to a perturbed version of the same matrix. Singular values are nonnegative numbers whose squares are the eigenvalues of \(A^*A\) (with the usual rectangular truncation). A numerical rank threshold counts singular values strictly greater than the chosen cutoff. With \(\epsilon=10^{-8}\), the script computes a smallest singular value of approximately \(2.4254\times10^{-9}\). The cutoff \(10^{-10}\) gives numerical rank three, and \(10^{-6}\) gives numerical rank two. In a measurement problem, the cutoff must reflect the matrix scaling and the level of error in the model.

Exercises with worked solutions

Construct a compatibility certificate

For the matrix in (2.1), test \(b=(3,2,7)^T\). Could restricting the actuators to any subset make this target attainable?

Solution. The linear test \((-1,-1,1)^*b\) gives \(7-3-2=2\). It vanishes on \(\mathcal R(A)\), so the target is impossible. Restricting the actuators to a subset corresponds to composing \(A\) with an injection that sets the remaining coordinates to zero. The new image is a subspace of the old one, which retains the same incompatibility certificate.

Distinguish row reduction from a change of actuator coordinates

For (2.1), explain why reporting the first two columns of the reduced row echelon form as an output basis gives the wrong plane. Give a vector in that reported plane that fails the instrument constraint.

Solution. Those columns span the plane \(b_3=0\). The original image obeys \(b_3=b_1+b_2\). The reported plane contains \((1,0,0)^T\), which violates the constraint. The pivot indices identify which original columns to use, since the reduction operates by invertible recombination of sensor readings.

Find the entire solution set

Produce all commands for target \((1,2,3)^T\). Identify the unique solution with \(x_3=x_4=0\) and a different solution with \(x_4=1\).

Solution. Set \(x_3=u\) and \(x_4=v\) in \(s=1\), \(t=2\). This gives \((1-u-2v,\,2-u+v,\,u,\,v)^T\). Taking \(u=v=0\) gives \((1,2,0,0)^T\). Taking \(u=0\) and \(v=1\) gives \((-1,3,0,1)^T\). The difference \((-2,1,0,1)^T\) is the second kernel basis vector.

Design a second sensor experiment

A second experiment supplies one additional measurement row \(h^*\), where \(h\in\mathbb R^4\). Characterize when this measurement reduces the invisible input dimension.

Solution. The new kernel is \(\ker A\cap\ker h^*\). If \(h\in\mathcal R(A^*)\), then (2.3) makes \(h^*\) identically zero on \(\ker A\). If \(h\notin\mathcal R(A^*)\), its component in \(\ker A\) is nonzero, and pairing it with that component yields a positive squared length. Thus the restriction of \(h^*\) to \(\ker A\) has rank one. Rank–nullity on this two-dimensional space yields a one-dimensional new kernel. Measuring \(x_4\) is one choice that reduces the invisible dimension, since the second basis vector of \(\ker A\) has fourth component one.

Diagnose rank loss without forming the product

Suppose \(\operatorname{rank}A=3\) for \(A\in\mathbb R^{4\times5}\) and \(\operatorname{rank}B=4\) for \(B\in\mathbb R^{5\times4}\). Give the possible ranks of \(AB\) and the condition for the lower rank.

Solution. The lower bound is \(3+4-5=2\) and the upper bound is three. By (2.4), rank two occurs exactly when \(\dim(\mathcal R(B)\cap\ker A)=2\), equivalently when the two-dimensional \(\ker A\) lies in \(\mathcal R(B)\). If \(\ker A=\operatorname{span}\{e_4,e_5\}\), then \(\operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_2,e_4,e_5\}\) realizes the lower rank as the image of \(B\), while \(\operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4\}\) realizes the upper rank. Here \(e_j\) denotes coordinate vector \(j\) of \(\mathbb R^5\).

Sources and limits

The course sequence is Lecture 5, fundamental subspaces, Lecture 6, the fundamental theorem, and Lecture 7, properties of rank. The compatibility and rank proofs assume exact finite-dimensional linear data. In an instrument with noise, a failed compatibility check can signal measurement error or model error. The subspaces identify the offending direction, while a statistical or deterministic error model is needed to judge the size of the discrepancy.
