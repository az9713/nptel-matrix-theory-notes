# Spectral radius and convergent matrix powers

Two estimates in a calibration routine are updated repeatedly. An error in the second estimate leaks into the first before it decays. If one update magnifies the error, can repeated updates still converge? A spectral radius below one answers the eventual question. An induced norm and the direction of the initial error can expose much larger errors during the early updates.

## Spectral radius measures eigenvector amplification

[L23 02:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Am_nmqS7SPs&t=120s)

For a square matrix $B$, an eigenvalue $\lambda$ is a scalar for which $Bv=\lambda v$ for some nonzero eigenvector $v$. Take eigenvalues over $\mathbb C$ even if $B$ is real. Write $\rho(B)=\max_\lambda|\lambda|$ for the spectral radius. On an eigenvector, $B^kv=\lambda^kv$, so $\rho(B)$ is the largest amplification per application along a single eigenvector. For any complex induced norm, $\|Bv\|=|\lambda|\|v\|$ gives $\rho(B)\leq\|B\|$.

The same lower bound holds for any submultiplicative norm $M$ on the real or complex matrix space. By fixed-dimensional norm equivalence, the usual spectral norm on matrices satisfies $\|X\|_2\leq cM(X)$ for a constant $c>0$ and all $X$. For a real matrix, the spectral norm is unchanged by allowing complex inputs because the eigenvalues of $X^*X$ give either maximum. Thus $\rho(B)^k\leq\|B^k\|_2\leq cM(B)^k$ for each positive integer $k$. Taking $k$th roots and using $c^{1/k}\to1$ yields the claim.

Use the original calibration-update matrix

$$
B=\begin{pmatrix}0.8&4\\0&0.8\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{7.1}
$$

An affine update has the form $x_{k+1}=Bx_k+b$, where $b$ is constant. If $x_\star$ is a fixed point, then the error $e_k=x_k-x_\star$ obeys $e_{k+1}=Be_k$. The matrix is triangular and has the sole eigenvalue $0.8$. But applying it to $e_0=(0,1)^T$ gives $(4,0.8)^T$, a gain of four in the maximum norm. Write $B=0.8I+H$, where $H$ consists only of the off-diagonal four. Since $H^2=0$, the binomial expansion gives

$$
B^k=0.8^kI+k\,0.8^{k-1}H\qquad(k\geq1).
\tag{7.2}
$$

The second term can dominate at early iterations. Its magnitude eventually vanishes because exponential decay beats the factor $k$, as can be checked by taking the ratio of consecutive terms, which tends to $0.8$. A test based only on eigenvectors misses the cross-coordinate term in this example.

## A norm adapted to one matrix

[L24 03:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xTPi795hK1w&t=216s)

For every complex $B$ and every $\eta>0$, there is an induced norm in which $\|B\|\leq\rho(B)+\eta$. To see the construction, first obtain an upper triangular form $T=Q^*BQ$ with unitary $Q$. Choose a unit eigenvector as the first column of a unitary basis. The first column of the transformed matrix then has a zero block below the eigenvalue. Repeat this operation on the smaller block in the remaining coordinates, which gives triangular form by induction. The diagonal of $T$ contains the eigenvalues, as shown by the factorization of $\det(\lambda I-T)$ as a product of diagonal terms.

Let $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,d,d^2,\ldots,d^{n-1})$ with $0<d<1$. For $j>i$, the $(i,j)$ entry of $D^{-1}TD$ equals $d^{j-i}t_{ij}$, and the diagonal remains unchanged. Each off-diagonal row sum tends to zero as $d\downarrow0$. For $d$ sufficiently small, the largest row sum of $D^{-1}TD$ is at most $\rho(B)+\eta$. This is the induced norm of $B$ associated with $N(x)=\|D^{-1}Q^*x\|_\infty$, because substitution $z=D^{-1}Q^*x$ gives the coordinate matrix $D^{-1}TD$. For a real matrix the same complex-space estimate applies to all real starting vectors.

In (7.1), take $Q=I_2$ and $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,0.02)$. The rescaled off-diagonal entry is $0.08$, so the new maximum row-sum norm is $0.88$. The coordinate budget is $N(x)=\max(|x_1|,50|x_2|)$. The second-coordinate input with ordinary maximum length one now has length 50. The new norm proves contraction by charging much more for an error in the leaking direction. It leaves the original transient trajectory (7.2) intact.

## Power convergence and the Neumann series

[L25 01:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PVGeabmeLDQ&t=96s) · [L25 22:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PVGeabmeLDQ&t=1353s)

If $\rho(B)<1$, choose $\eta>0$ so $\rho(B)+\eta<1$. The adapted norm and submultiplicativity give $\|B^k\|\leq(\rho(B)+\eta)^k$. Equivalence transfers the convergence to zero to any fixed finite-dimensional norm. Conversely, if $B^k\to0$, applying the powers to an eigenvector gives $\lambda^kv\to0$, forcing $|\lambda|<1$ for every eigenvalue. The same argument with $k$th roots gives the spectral-radius formula. For any submultiplicative $M$,

$$
\lim_{k\to\infty}M(B^k)^{1/k}=\rho(B).
\tag{7.3}
$$

For the lower bound use $\rho(B)^k=\rho(B^k)$, which follows by taking powers of the triangular form, and $\rho(B^k)\leq M(B^k)$. For the upper bound, norm equivalence gives $M(B^k)\leq c_\eta(\rho(B)+\eta)^k$ from the adapted norm. After taking roots the constant disappears. Let $\eta$ decrease to zero. The constant before taking roots can be large, so (7.3) describes the asymptotic rate.

Suppose $q=\|B\|<1$ in an induced norm and let $S_m=\sum_{k=0}^m B^k$. The series is absolutely convergent in this norm because $\|B^k\|\leq q^k$ and finite-dimensional normed spaces are complete. Completeness here follows from norm equivalence and coordinatewise completeness of the scalar field. The finite telescoping identity $(I-B)S_m=I-B^{m+1}$ gives $(I-B)\lim_m S_m=I$, proving

$$
(I-B)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty B^k,\qquad
\|(I-B)^{-1}-S_m\|\leq\frac{q^{m+1}}{1-q}.
\tag{7.4}
$$

The tail bound is a scalar geometric sum. The series still converges whenever $\rho(B)<1$, since there is then a norm with $q<1$. Conversely, convergence of the series forces its terms to vanish, which requires $\rho(B)<1$. The inverse may exist beyond this regime. For $B=2I$, the inverse of $I-B$ is $-I$, but the series terms grow. In Banach's inverse lemma, a matrix $A$ close to $I$ with $\|I-A\|<1$ has this inverse series with $B=I-A$.

For the example, solving $(I-B)x_\star=b$ gives $x_\star=(100,5)^T$ when $b=(0,1)^T$. The large first coordinate comes from summing the leakage terms in (7.2) across all powers. Because each update's additive term enters again, a convergent iteration can have a large steady response. For a finite run from $x_0=0$, the tail estimate (7.4) applies to the sum of the first $m+1$ powers acting on $b$, and the absolute-coordinate bound requires converting the adapted norm to the original coordinate budget.

## Strict diagonal dominance as an inverse certificate

[L26 20:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WsO_s8dNfVI&t=1202s)

A square matrix $A=(a_{ij})$ is strictly row diagonally dominant if $|a_{ii}|>\sum_{j\ne i}|a_{ij}|$ for every row $i$. Let $D_A$ be its diagonal matrix of diagonal entries. Every diagonal entry is nonzero, so $D_A$ is invertible. Form $B_A=I-D_A^{-1}A$. Its diagonal is zero, and the absolute sum of off-diagonal entries in each row is less than one by strict dominance. Therefore $\|B_A\|_\infty<1$ and (7.4) makes $I-B_A=D_A^{-1}A$ invertible. This proves the Lévy–Desplanques theorem with the row-sum mechanism exposed. Column dominance also suffices by applying the row result to $A^T$. The update $x_{k+1}=B_Ax_k+D_A^{-1}b$ is Jacobi iteration for $Ax=b$, obtained by solving each row for its own coordinate using the previous values of other coordinates. It converges from every initial state under strict dominance because successive errors are multiplied by $B_A$.

For $A=\begin{pmatrix}4&-1\\-2&5\end{pmatrix}$, the row ratios are $1/4$ and $2/5$, so $\|B_A\|_\infty=2/5$. If $b=(1,3)^T$, the exact solution is $(4/9,7/9)^T$. The error after $k$ Jacobi updates is at most $(2/5)^k\|e_0\|_\infty$. Weak dominance, with non-strict inequalities, permits the singular matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Invertibility alone does not imply dominance, as the coordinate-swap matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ demonstrates.

## Exercises with worked solutions

### Spectral radius can vanish without a zero matrix

Let $H=\begin{pmatrix}0&4\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. Compute its radius and show that $\rho$ fails the triangle inequality.

**Solution.** Both eigenvalues of $H$ and $H^T$ are zero by triangularity. Their sum has eigenvectors $(1,1)^T$ and $(1,-1)^T$ with eigenvalues $4$ and $-4$. The radius of the sum is four, while the sum of radii is zero. Nonzero $H$ with zero radius also violates definiteness.

### Locate the transient peak

For (7.1), find the largest value of the first component generated by $e_0=(0,1)^T$.

**Solution.** For $k\geq1$ the first component is $4k\,0.8^{k-1}$. The ratio when increasing $k$ to $k+1$ is $0.8(1+1/k)$, greater than one for $k<4$, equal to one for $k=4$, and less than one for $k>4$. The peaks at steps four and five both equal $8.192$.

### Choose a scaling for contraction

For $B$ in (7.1), which $d>0$ makes the row sum of $D^{-1}BD$ strictly less than one for $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,d)$?

**Solution.** The first row sum is $0.8+4d$ and the second is $0.8$. Thus contraction requires $0<d<0.05$. At $d=0.05$ the resulting norm bound is one, while the spectral radius and the dynamics retain their original values.

### Check the boundary of the inverse expansion

For $B=-I$, does $\sum_{k=0}^\infty B^k$ converge, and does $I-B$ have an inverse?

**Solution.** The partial sums alternate between $I$ and zero and do not converge. The inverse of $I-B=2I$ exists and equals $\tfrac12 I$. A convergent inverse expansion requires the stricter radius condition $\rho(B)<1$.

### Certify a Jacobi iteration budget

For the strictly dominant $A$ above, start with $x_0=0$ and use the upper bound $\|e_0\|_\infty\leq1$. How many iterations suffice to guarantee maximum error at most $10^{-4}$?

**Solution.** The estimate requires $(2/5)^k\leq10^{-4}$. Step ten gives $(2/5)^{10}=0.0001048576$, just above the target. Step eleven multiplies that by $2/5$ and gives $0.00004194304$. Eleven steps therefore suffice by the stated bound.

## Sources and limits

[NPTEL Matrix Theory](https://www.nptel.ac.in/courses/111108157) Lectures 23–26 supply the radius, convergence, inverse, and dominance topics. The coupled calibration update is an original construction. [Murthy’s companion IISc course index](https://ece.iisc.ac.in/~cmurthy/e2-212-matrix-theory-video-lectures-and-notes-2/) lists the parallel treatment of spectral radius, Banach's lemma, and diagonal dominance. All power and inverse guarantees here assume a fixed finite square matrix. Replacing $B$ by a time-dependent sequence of matrices would require control of the resulting products, for example by a common induced norm under which every update is uniformly contractive.
