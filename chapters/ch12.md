# Cayley–Hamilton, normal matrices, and QR form

An actuator repeatedly receives a fixed command while its internal state evolves. The response must settle below a design limit. A short polynomial identity computes the steady state, and normality determines when eigenvalues alone can certify the size of that response. An orthogonal factorization provides a way to solve the equations numerically.

The running model extends the original example of [Chapter 11](ch11.html). With state $x_k\in\mathbb C^2$, set $x_{k+1}=Ax_k+b$ with $A=\begin{bmatrix}0.8&3\\0&0.5\end{bmatrix}$ and command $b=e_2=(0,1)^T$. A fixed point $x$ satisfies $(I_2-A)x=b$. The example and its numerical checks are original.

## A matrix satisfies its characteristic polynomial ([L41 00:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WROFJ15hk00&t=37s))

Let $M$ be a square matrix of order $n$ and $p_M(t)=\det(tI_n-M)=\sum_{j=0}^n p_jt^j$. The Cayley–Hamilton theorem asserts $p_M(M)=\sum_{j=0}^n p_jM^j=0$, with $M^0=I_n$. Matrix polynomials use scalar coefficients and their multiplication is consistent with substitution. The adjugate proof must handle matrix-valued coefficients in an intermediate expression carefully.

Let $C(t)=\operatorname{adj}(tI_n-M)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} C_jt^j$, where the adjugate is the transpose of the cofactor matrix. The cofactor identity gives $C(t)(tI_n-M)=p_M(t)I_n$. Coefficient comparison gives $p_jI_n=C_{j-1}-C_jM$ for $0\leq j\leq n$, setting $C_{-1}=C_n=0$. Multiply each identity on the right by $M^j$ and sum. The terms $C_{j-1}M^j$ and $-C_jM^{j+1}$ telescope, so the sum is $p_M(M)=0$. The orientation of the coefficients makes the cancellation valid without assuming arbitrary coefficient matrices commute.

The Schur form gives a second proof. If $T$ is upper triangular with $k$th diagonal entry $\lambda_k$, let $F_k$ be the span of the first $k$ coordinate vectors and $F_0=\{0\}$. Then $(T-\lambda_k I_n)F_k\subset F_{k-1}$. Applying these factors from index $n$ down to one kills every vector. As all the factors are polynomials in $T$, their product is $p_M(T)$. Unitary similarity transfers the identity to $M$.

For $A$, the polynomial is $t^2-1.3t+0.4$. Thus $A^{k+2}=1.3A^{k+1}-0.4A^k$ for each integer $k\ge0$, and every power is a linear combination of $I_2$ and $A$. Direct multiplication gives the upper-right entry of $A^2$ as $0.8(3)+3(0.5)=3.9$, agreeing with the upper-right entry of $1.3A-0.4I_2$. The same scalar recurrence governs every entry while retaining coupling in its initial values.

## Inverse formulas and a diagonalizability criterion ([L42 06:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hTV-BL79c6g&t=391s))

If a scalar $s$ is outside the spectrum, define $q_s(t)=(p_M(s)-p_M(t))/(s-t)$, a polynomial because the numerator vanishes at $t=s$. Evaluating $(s-t)q_s(t)=p_M(s)-p_M(t)$ at $M$ gives
$$
(sI_n-M)^{-1}=\frac{q_s(M)}{p_M(s)}.
$$
This inverse at shift $s$ is called the resolvent. In the actuator model $s=1$ and $q_s(t)=s+t-1.3$. The fixed point is $(A-0.3I_2)b/0.1=(30,2)^T$. The eigenvalue distances from $1$ are $0.2$ and $0.5$. The response norm $\sqrt{904}$ is much larger than the inverse of the smaller distance. The off-diagonal transfer contributes a factor $3/(0.2\cdot0.5)$ in the first coordinate. For large matrices, solving a factored system avoids the effort of forming all entries of the inverse.

An annihilating polynomial $f$ satisfies $f(M)=0$. Suppose $f(t)=\prod_{j=1}^r(t-\lambda_j)$ with distinct roots in the working field. Form
$$
\ell_i(t)=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{t-\lambda_j}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}.
$$
The degree of $\sum_i\ell_i-1$ is at most $r-1$ and it vanishes at all $r$ roots, so $\sum_i\ell_i=1$. Each $(t-\lambda_i)\ell_i(t)$ is a scalar multiple of $f$. Evaluating at $M$ shows that $\ell_i(M)x\in\ker(M-\lambda_iI_n)$. Since $x=\sum_i\ell_i(M)x$, the eigenspaces span. Conversely, if $M$ has an eigenbasis, the product over its distinct eigenvalues annihilates it by evaluation on that basis. A diagonalizable matrix can have a characteristic polynomial with repeated roots. The requirement concerns existence of a simple-rooted annihilating polynomial.

## Normality and diagonal form ([L43 00:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WrgR50mKhuA&t=36s), [L44 01:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3jztEby7SHY&t=61s))

A square complex matrix $M$ is normal if $M^*M=MM^*$, with $M^*$ the adjoint. Squared Euclidean norms give $\|Mx\|_2^2=x^*M^*Mx$ and $\|M^*x\|_2^2=x^*MM^*x$. Normality makes these equal. The converse follows by testing coordinate vectors and two-coordinate sums with relative phases $1$ and $i$ in the Hermitian difference, as in [Chapter 11](ch11.html). Shifting a normal matrix by $\lambda I_n$ preserves normality because all extra terms in the product difference cancel. Thus $(M-\lambda I_n)v=0$ gives $(M^*-\overline\lambda I_n)v=0$, since the norms of the two shifted actions agree.

To prove the complex normal spectral theorem, use the unitary Schur representation $M=ZTZ^*$. Normality transfers to $T$ by cancellation of $Z^*Z$. The first column of upper triangular $T$ has only its diagonal entry, while the first row may contain other entries. Normality equates their squared lengths through the $(1,1)$ entry of $T^*T=TT^*$. Thus the off-diagonal entries of the first row vanish. The remaining diagonal block is also normal, so repetition makes $T$ diagonal. Conversely a unitary conjugate of a diagonal matrix is normal because the diagonal commutes with its adjoint. The squared Frobenius norm condition from [Chapter 11](ch11.html), $\|M\|_F^2=\sum_i|\lambda_i|^2$, is therefore equivalent to normality. The difference is the sum of squared strict upper entries of the Schur triangle, and it vanishes precisely when that triangle is diagonal. The spectral theorem covers Hermitian matrices, defined by $M=M^*$, and square unitary matrices, defined by $M^*M=MM^*=I_n$.

For normal $M$ and $s$ outside its spectrum, the resolvent has induced Euclidean norm $\max_i|s-\lambda_i|^{-1}$. In the unitary basis, it scales each coordinate by $1/(s-\lambda_i)$, so summing squares gives the upper bound and selecting a dominant coordinate attains it. For the actuator $A$, the first column has norm $0.8$ but the first row has norm $\sqrt{0.64+9}$. Hence $A$ is nonnormal and the eigenvalue distances do not give the normal-matrix response certificate. The distinct roots do give diagonalizability by a general invertible matrix.

## QR factorization and its relation to similarity ([L45 00:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Su3UwxTjEGQ&t=32s))

A tall matrix $M\in\mathbb C^{m\times n}$ with $m\geq n$ and independent columns admits $M=QR$, with $Q$ of size $m\times n$ satisfying $Q^*Q=I_n$ and $R$ upper triangular of order $n$ with positive real diagonal. Let $m_j$ denote column $j$. Subtract $\sum_{i<j}q_i(q_i^*m_j)$ from $m_j$ and divide by the residual norm to obtain $q_j$. Column independence makes the residual nonzero. The coefficients $r_{ij}=q_i^*m_j$ for $i<j$ and the positive residual norm $r_{jj}$ reconstruct $m_j$. For a second such factorization $M=\widetilde Q\widetilde R$, the square change between the two orthonormal bases is $R\widetilde R^{-1}$, which is upper triangular, unitary, and has positive diagonal. Its inverse is upper triangular by substitution and equals its lower triangular adjoint, so it is diagonal. Its positive diagonal entries are one, proving uniqueness.

If the column remainder is zero, choose any unit $q_j$ orthogonal to $q_1,\ldots,q_{j-1}$ and set $r_{jj}=0$. Such a vector exists because $j\leq n\leq m$. The earlier $q_i$ already reconstruct column $j$, and the construction continues. This gives a factorization for rank-deficient tall matrices, where the diagonal can include zeros and the factorization can be nonunique.

The actuator $A$ is already triangular with positive diagonal, so its square QR factorization is $Q=I_2$ and $R=A$. In a rotated sensor basis the matrix need not be triangular. Factorizing the fixed-point coefficient $I_2-A$ by QR transforms the equation to $Rx=Q^*b$. Applying a full unitary left transformation preserves the Euclidean norm of the residual. LAPACK usually represents the full unitary factor by a product of reflectors, which can be applied without forming the full matrix. [LAPACK Users’ Guide, QR Factorization](https://www.netlib.org/lapack/lug/node40.html)

For square $M=QR$, reversing the factors gives $RQ=Q^*MQ$. Thus this reversal defines a unitary similarity step. QR iteration repeatedly uses the identity to compute spectral forms. Similarity alone supplies no convergence claim. For example, the real quarter-turn is orthogonal and its positive-diagonal QR form has $R=I_2$. Reversing the factors leaves it unchanged. A real Schur representation can keep the rotation as a two-dimensional block.

The Schur calculation also links normality and canonical forms. A canonical form for a specified relation provides the same representative for all equivalent matrices after a convention such as ordering has been fixed. Complex normal matrices have a diagonal form under unitary similarity, unique once a common order of the eigenvalues is chosen. General complex matrices admit Schur triangles, but fixing the diagonal order does not make those triangles canonical. For example, conjugating a triangular $2\times2$ matrix by $\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)$ reverses the sign of its off-diagonal entry while leaving its diagonal fixed. Exact similarity has a different canonical form, developed in [Chapter 13](ch13.html).

## Exercises and solutions

1. Derive a formula for $M^{-1}$ using the characteristic polynomial when $M$ is invertible.<br>
   **Solution.** The constant term $p_0=(-1)^n\det M$ is nonzero. Multiply $p_M(M)=0$ by $M^{-1}$ and isolate $p_0M^{-1}$ to get $M^{-1}=-(\sum_{j=1}^n p_j M^{j-1})/p_0$.

2. Decompose a state into eigenspaces of a matrix satisfying $M^2=M$.<br>
   **Solution.** Since $M(I_n-M)=0$, $(I_n-M)x\in\ker M$. Since $(M-I_n)M=0$, $Mx\in\ker(M-I_n)$. Their sum is $x$. This realizes the simple-root criterion with the polynomial $t(t-1)$.

3. Find the complete normality condition for $\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\0&d\end{bmatrix}$.<br>
   **Solution.** Equal row and column norms at the first index force $|c|^2=0$. Conversely $c=0$ makes the matrix diagonal and normal. The values of $a,d$ can be any complex numbers.

4. Derive the least-squares solution for a tall full-column-rank $M=QR$.<br>
   **Solution.** Complete the columns of $Q$ by orthonormal columns $Q_\perp$. Applying the full adjoint basis matrix to $Mx-b$ produces blocks $Rx-Q^*b$ and $-Q_\perp^*b$. The second is independent of $x$, while the first vanishes uniquely when $Rx=Q^*b$ since $R$ is invertible.

Cayley–Hamilton reduces matrix polynomials to finite degree. Normality controls when simple spectral expressions provide exact Euclidean bounds, and QR supplies an orthogonal route to solving the coefficient equations.
