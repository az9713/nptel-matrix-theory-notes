# Vector norms and equivalence

The instrument from [Chapter 2](ch02.html) reports $(2,1,4)^T$ when compatible outputs must have the form $(s,t,s+t)^T$. Should the correction minimize total absolute adjustment, squared adjustment, or the worst adjustment in any channel? The three choices agree on which sequences of reading errors tend to zero in fixed dimension. They give different decisions for a finite error of one unit.

## What a norm measures

[L13 05:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iA7MnBhkXvQ&t=336s)

A norm on a vector space $V$ over $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$ is a function $N:V\to[0,\infty)$ satisfying $N(x)=0$ exactly at $x=0$, $N(\alpha x)=|\alpha|N(x)$ for every scalar $\alpha$, and $N(x+y)\leq N(x)+N(y)$. These conditions express definiteness, absolute homogeneity, and the triangle inequality. From the triangle inequality, $N(x)\leq N(y)+N(x-y)$, and interchanging $x,y$ gives the reverse triangle inequality

$$
|N(x)-N(y)|\leq N(x-y).
\tag{5.1}
$$

Thus changes in the reported magnitude are controlled by the magnitude of the underlying change. The **unit ball** is $\{x:N(x)\leq1\}$. For any two vectors in this ball and any $\theta\in[0,1]$, their mixture $\theta x+(1-\theta)y$ also lies there by homogeneity and the triangle inequality. This property is convexity. It matters for feasibility because averaging two errors that each pass a norm threshold preserves that threshold.

Norms can also be constructed from simpler norms. For instance if $M$ is a norm on the output space of an injective linear map $T$, then $N(x)=M(Tx)$ inherits homogeneity and the triangle inequality from linearity and the norm axioms. Injectivity supplies definiteness. This covers positive channel rescaling before applying a coordinate norm. With a zero scale a purely unmeasured coordinate could have zero reported size. [L14 25:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3XOM2g53bNI&t=1538s)

The length associated with an inner product is a norm by the Cauchy–Schwarz and triangle arguments in [Chapter 3](ch03.html). Each such norm satisfies the parallelogram law

$$
N(x+y)^2+N(x-y)^2=2N(x)^2+2N(y)^2.
\tag{5.2}
$$

Indeed, the two squares expand into the same diagonal terms and cross terms with opposite signs. For the norm $\|x\|_1=\sum_j|x_j|$ on $\mathbb R^2$, $x=(1,0)^T$ and $y=(0,1)^T$ give left side eight and right side four. For the norm $\|x\|_\infty=\max_j|x_j|$ they give left side two and right side four. Definiteness and the triangle inequality therefore describe more geometries than inner products do. The converse of (5.2) is the Jordan–von Neumann characterization. [Jordan and von Neumann, 1935](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1968653)

## Coordinate norms and their triangle inequality

[L14 11:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3XOM2g53bNI&t=711s)

For $1\leq p<\infty$ and $x\in\mathbb F^n$, define

$$
\|x\|_p=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_j|^p\right)^{1/p},
\qquad \|x\|_\infty=\max_j|x_j|.
\tag{5.3}
$$

The endpoint cases $p=1$ and $p=\infty$ obey the triangle inequality by applying the scalar absolute-value triangle inequality before summing or taking a maximum. For $1<p<\infty$ let $q=p/(p-1)$, so $1/p+1/q=1$. For nonnegative $a,b$, weighted arithmetic–geometric mean gives $ab\leq a^p/p+b^q/q$. This follows by applying concavity of the logarithm to $a^p$ and $b^q$ with weights $1/p$ and $1/q$, and extends to zero arguments by continuity. Normalize nonzero vectors $u,v$ by their $p$- and $q$-lengths and apply this inequality entry by entry. The sum of the upper bounds is $1/p+1/q=1$, which proves Hölder's inequality

$$
\sum_j|u_jv_j|\leq\|u\|_p\|v\|_q.
\tag{5.4}
$$

To prove the triangle inequality let $z=u+v$. Bound $\sum_j|z_j|^p$ by $\sum_j(|u_j|+|v_j|)|z_j|^{p-1}$. Hölder's inequality bounds the two terms by $(\|u\|_p+\|v\|_p)\|z\|_p^{p-1}$ because $(p-1)q=p$. If $z\ne0$, division gives Minkowski's inequality $\|u+v\|_p\leq\|u\|_p+\|v\|_p$. The case $z=0$ is automatic. Definiteness and homogeneity follow from (5.3), completing the norm proof. The same expression for $0<p<1$ fails on two distinct coordinate vectors, whose sum has length $2^{1/p}>2$.

For $1\leq p<q\leq\infty$, the sharp dimension-dependent comparison is

$$
\|x\|_q\leq\|x\|_p\leq n^{1/p-1/q}\|x\|_q,
\qquad 1/\infty=0.
\tag{5.5}
$$

For the first inequality divide a nonzero $x$ by $\|x\|_p$. Each resulting magnitude is at most one, so raising the exponent from $p$ to $q$ decreases the sum of powers. This gives $\|x\|_q/\|x\|_p\leq1$, including the maximum as the endpoint. For the second, when $q$ is finite apply Hölder to $\sum_j|x_j|^p\cdot1$ with conjugate exponents $q/p$ and $q/(q-p)$, then take the $p$th root. When $q=\infty$, bound each summand by $\|x\|_\infty^p$. A coordinate vector attains the first bound, and a vector with every magnitude equal and positive attains the second. In particular $\|x\|_\infty\leq\|x\|_p\leq n^{1/p}\|x\|_\infty$, which proves the limit as $p\to\infty$.

## Correction objectives for the same reading

[L15 07:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UkYKyelb5Wc&t=420s)

Return to the incompatible observation $b=(2,1,4)^T$. Let $z$ be any real compatible reading and $r=b-z$ its residual. With $y=(-1,-1,1)^T$, compatibility of $z$ requires $y^Tr=1$. In particular $\sum_j|r_j|\geq1$. A residual $(0,0,1)^T$ attains equality, and so does any residual with $r_1,r_2\leq0$, $r_3\geq0$, and $-r_1-r_2+r_3=1$. Minimizing $\|r\|_1$ permits many residuals including concentrated correction.

Cauchy–Schwarz bounds $1=y^Tr\leq\sqrt3\|r\|_2$, with equality for $r=y/3$. This selects the unique least-squares fit previously established by orthogonal projection. Finally, $1=y^Tr\leq3\|r\|_\infty$ gives the same $r=y/3$ for the worst-channel criterion. Equality forces all three signed summands to be $1/3$. The two objectives coincide in this case because the entries of the certificate $y$ have equal magnitude. For a relation $y=(-2,-1,1)^T$ with the same discrepancy $y^Tr=1$, the minimum Euclidean residual is $y/6$ while the minimum maximum residual is $(-1,-1,1)^T/4$. Weighted penalties or a different compatibility relation can separate the estimates even when all residual norms are equivalent.

## Equivalence on a fixed finite-dimensional space

[L15 24:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UkYKyelb5Wc&t=1441s) · [L16 00:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TWHfGPyHyAI&t=32s)

Two norms $N_1,N_2$ are equivalent when there exist constants $c,C>0$ with $cN_1(x)\leq N_2(x)\leq CN_1(x)$ for every $x$. On a fixed finite-dimensional space take basis coordinates and let $e_j$ denote the vector with a single one in coordinate $j$. By repeated triangle inequalities any norm $N$ obeys

$$
N(x)\leq\sum_j |x_j|N(e_j)\leq M\|x\|_\infty,
\qquad M=\sum_jN(e_j).
\tag{5.6}
$$

Equation (5.1) then proves continuity of $N$ in the maximum norm. The set $\{x:\|x\|_\infty=1\}$ is closed and bounded in finite-dimensional real coordinates, with $\mathbb C^n$ identified with $\mathbb R^{2n}$. It is compact, so $N$ attains a minimum $m$ on it. Each point is nonzero, hence $m>0$. Apply this minimum to $x/\|x\|_\infty$ to obtain $m\|x\|_\infty\leq N(x)$. Since every norm is bounded above and below by multiples of the coordinate maximum norm, division of the corresponding constants compares any two norms. The zero-dimensional case has only the zero vector and satisfies the comparison trivially.

For a sequence $x^{(k)}$, convergence to $x$ in norm $N$ means $N(x^{(k)}-x)\to0$. The two-sided comparison shows that convergence in any one of the norms implies convergence to the same $x$ in all others. A finite maximum of convergent coordinate errors vanishes, so this also gives equivalence with componentwise convergence. The constants can grow with dimension. On the space of finitely supported sequences, the vector with first $k$ entries equal to $1/k$ and remaining entries zero has maximum norm $1/k$ and sum norm one. There is no fixed comparison constant bounding the sum norm by a multiple of the maximum on that infinite-dimensional space.

## Exercises with worked solutions

### Test a cost that resembles a norm

Does $N(x)=x_1^2+x_2^2$ define a norm on $\mathbb R^2$?

**Solution.** It is positive on nonzero vectors, but $N(2e_1)=4$ and $2N(e_1)=2$. It also fails the triangle inequality for $x=y=e_1$. Taking the square root supplies the Euclidean norm.

### Locate a worst coefficient for the norm comparison

Find the smallest $C$ for which $\|x\|_1\leq C\|x\|_2$ in $\mathbb R^9$.

**Solution.** Equation (5.5) gives $C\leq3$. For the vector of nine ones the norms are nine and three, giving ratio three. Any smaller constant fails there.

### Measure a different compatibility relation

For $y=(-2,-1,1)^T$ and $y^Tr=1$, find all sum-norm minimizers.

**Solution.** The inequality $1=y^Tr\leq2\|r\|_1$ gives a bound of $1/2$. For equality, only coordinates where $|y_i|=2$ may carry residual. The first coordinate is the only such coordinate, and satisfying the equality requires $r=(-1/2,0,0)^T$.

### Recognize a weighted maximum

Given positive tolerances $\tau_j$, define $N_\tau(r)=\max_j|r_j|/\tau_j$. Give sharp comparison constants with $\|r\|_\infty$.

**Solution.** Every ratio is at most $\|r\|_\infty/\min_j\tau_j$, proving the upper constant. Take an index attaining $\|r\|_\infty$. Its ratio is at least $\|r\|_\infty/\max_j\tau_j$, proving the lower constant. Coordinate vectors at indices with smallest and largest tolerance attain the respective bounds. The norm tests the largest fraction of a tolerance used anywhere.

### Identify where the convergence proof needs finite dimension

For $x^{(k)}$ with $k$ entries equal to $1/k$, find the $p$-norm as $k\to\infty$. Which norms assign convergence to zero?

**Solution.** For finite $p$, $\|x^{(k)}\|_p=(k/k^p)^{1/p}=k^{1/p-1}$. This is one at $p=1$ and tends to zero for $p>1$. The maximum is $1/k$, so it also tends to zero. The increasingly many small coordinates create a fixed total absolute error.
