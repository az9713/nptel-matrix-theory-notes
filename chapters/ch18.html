Chapter 18 · Lectures 69–73

Locating eigenvalues and bounding their movement

L69 L70 L71 L72 L73

A controller passes its nominal stability test, but its couplings were calibrated from noisy data. An engineer needs a certificate that every mode still decays. For a continuous-time linear system \(\dot x=Ax\), strict negativity of the real part of every eigenvalue implies asymptotic decay: in Jordan coordinates each component is a linear combination of exponentials multiplied by polynomials. A matrix with eigenvalues in the left half-plane can still show transient growth, so the certificate addresses the long-time behavior.

Use the original model

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}-2&4&0\\0&-3&0\\0&0&-8\end{pmatrix}.\]

Its eigenvalues are \(-2,-3,-8\) because it is triangular. A measured feedback term changes the lower-left entry of the leading block from zero to \(t\). Write the perturbed generator as \(A+tE\) with \(E=e_2e_1^T\), where \(e_j\) is coordinate vector \(j\). Time units are fixed throughout the example. The feedback closes the path between the first two variables even though its matrix entry can be small.

Dominant diagonals certify invertibility

L69 10:06

For a complex square matrix \(M=(m_{ij})\) define its row radii \(r_i=\sum_{j\ne i}|m_{ij}|\). Strict row diagonal dominance means \(|m_{ii}|>r_i\) for every row. If \(Mx=0\) for some \(x\ne0\), choose \(i\) with \(|x_i|=\max_j|x_j|\). The row equation gives

\[|m_{ii}||x_i|\le\sum_{j\ne i}|m_{ij}||x_j| \le r_i|x_i|.\]

Since \(|x_i|>0\), this contradicts strict dominance and proves invertibility. Transposition gives the column version. Equality in all rows does not certify invertibility: \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\) has a null vector \((1,-1)^T\). The invertible permutation matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\) shows dominance is sufficient.

More quantitatively let \(\delta=\min_i(|m_{ii}|-r_i)>0\). For \(Mx=b\), taking a largest component of \(x\) gives \(\|b\|_\infty\ge\delta\|x\|_\infty\) by the reverse triangle inequality. Here \(\|x\|_\infty=\max_i|x_i|\). Thus the induced matrix norm of the inverse is at most \(1/\delta\). This also bounds the resolvent \((zI-M)^{-1}\) whenever a complex shift \(z\) makes \(zI-M\) strictly dominant.

In the controller coordinates the first row fails the test since \(2<4\). Scale the first coordinate using \(S=\operatorname{diag}(8,1,1)\). The similar matrix \(S^{-1}(A+tE)S\) has off-diagonal entries \(0.5\) and \(8t\) in the leading block. The coordinate transformation makes the original coupling smaller and the return coupling larger. Both must be included when quantifying robustness.

Gershgorin disks locate and count modes

L70 00:34 · L70 04:42 · L71 02:01

For row \(i\) let \(D_i=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z-m_{ii}|\le r_i\}\). A point outside their union makes \(zI-M\) strictly diagonally dominant, so every eigenvalue of \(M\) belongs to the union. Since \(M\) and \(M^T\) have the same characteristic polynomial, the eigenvalues also lie in the intersection of the row and column unions. Different rows or columns can account for the same eigenvalue.

A connected component of the union containing exactly \(k\) disks, counted by index, contains exactly \(k\) eigenvalues with algebraic multiplicity. To prove this, form \(M(s)=\operatorname{diag}(m_{ii})+s(M-\operatorname{diag}(m_{ii}))\) for \(s\in[0,1]\). The disks at \(s\) lie inside the original disks. At \(s=0\) each diagonal center contributes one root. Put disjoint neighborhoods around the original components and choose their boundaries in the spectral exclusion region between components. The number of roots inside each such boundary is constant because the characteristic polynomial changes continuously and has no zeros on the boundary. Equivalently its winding number around zero is constant under this homotopy. At \(s=1\) the component therefore still contains its original \(k\) roots. Counts can fail for an individual disk when its boundary overlaps other disks, since a root can move between them without leaving the union.

For \(t=0.02\), the scaled controller rows have centers \(-2,-3,-8\) and radii \(0.5,0.16,0\). The three disks are disjoint and each stays in the open left half-plane, so each has one eigenvalue. For all \(|t|<3/8\) the row disks certify stability under this fixed scaling, allowing overlaps when we only need the half-plane condition. For real \(t\) the leading block has characteristic polynomial \((z+2)(z+3)-4t\). Its two roots have real parts below zero exactly when \(6-4t>0\): real roots then have the same sign and sum to \(-5\), whereas conjugate roots have real part \(-5/2\). The disk certificate is conservative for this structured error.

Sensitivity of a simple mode

L71 22:01

For a simple eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of \(A\), let \(x\) and \(y\) be nonzero right and left eigenvectors, so \(Ax=\lambda x\) and \(y^*A=\lambda y^*\). Their inner product is nonzero. If it vanished, \(x\) would lie in the range of \(A-\lambda I\), which is the orthogonal complement of the left nullspace spanned by \(y\). A solution \(v\) of \((A-\lambda I)v=x\) would give a length-two Jordan chain, contradicting algebraic simplicity.

The characteristic root can be followed differentiably near \(t=0\) by the implicit function theorem for a simple polynomial root. Its eigenvector can be chosen differentiably there by fixing one normalization and solving for the remaining components using a nonsingular minor. Differentiate \((A+tE)x(t)=\lambda(t)x(t)\) at zero and multiply on the left by \(y^*\). The terms with \(x'(0)\) cancel, giving

\[\lambda'(0)=\frac{y^*Ex}{y^*x},\qquad |\lambda'(0)|\le \frac{\|y\|_2\|x\|_2}{|y^*x|}\,\|E\|_2 .\]

Here \(\|x\|_2=\sqrt{x^*x}\) and \(\|E\|_2\) is its induced matrix norm. The bound can be attained over arbitrary norm-one perturbation directions by an outer product of the normalized left and right eigenvectors. For the controller mode \(-2\) take \(x=e_1\) and \(y=(1,4,0)^T\). The condition factor is \(\sqrt{17}\) and the actual derivative for the return coupling is \(4\). At \(t=0.02\), the eigenvalue near \(-2\) is \((-5+\sqrt{1+16t})/2\), about \(-1.92554\). Its shift \(0.07446\) is close to the linear approximation \(0.08\). The derivative is local and depends on a simple root.

If the leading block is changed to \(\begin{pmatrix}-2&4\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}\), the same return coupling splits its repeated defective eigenvalue into \(-2\pm2\sqrt t\) for positive real \(t\). A Lipschitz estimate proportional to \(|t|\) fails as \(t\) tends to zero. This is a different sensitivity law even when both nominal eigenvalues are well inside the stable half-plane.

Bauer–Fike for a diagonalizable matrix

L72 00:16 · L72 16:00

Let \(A=V\Lambda V^{-1}\), where \(V\) is an invertible eigenvector matrix and \(\Lambda\) is diagonal. If \(\mu\) is an eigenvalue of \(A+F\) with eigenvector \(w\), set \(z=V^{-1}w\). If \(\mu\) is not an eigenvalue of \(A\), rearranging the eigenvector equation yields

\[z=(\mu I-\Lambda)^{-1}V^{-1}FVz .\]

Take norms and divide by \(\|z\|\). For an induced vector \(p\)-norm, the norm of the diagonal inverse is the reciprocal of \(\min_i|\mu-\lambda_i|\). Thus

\[\min_i|\mu-\lambda_i|\le\kappa_p(V)\|F\|_p,\qquad \kappa_p(V)=\|V\|_p\|V^{-1}\|_p.\]

If \(\mu\) already belongs to the old spectrum, the distance is zero. The argument permits repeated eigenvalues when the old matrix has a complete eigenbasis. The factor depends on that basis, whose columns may be rescaled. The theorem offers nearest-spectrum inclusion for each new eigenvalue, with no specified one-to-one labeling. Bauer and Fike, Norms and Exclusion Theorems.

For the controller take \(V=\begin{pmatrix}1&-4&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\). Its induced infinity norm, the maximum absolute row sum, is \(5\), as is the norm of its inverse. The radius for \(F=tE\) is therefore \(25|t|\). At \(t=0.02\) the inclusion radius is \(0.5\) and the actual largest distance is about \(0.07446\). If \(A\) is normal, meaning \(A^*A=AA^*\), the spectral theorem for normal matrices provides a unitary \(V\) and \(\kappa_2(V)=1\). The perturbation \(F\) need not preserve normality for this inclusion. If both matrices are Hermitian, the Weyl estimate additionally pairs their sorted real spectra.

Birkhoff and Hoffman–Wielandt give a global matching

L73 16:30 · L73 23:02

A real matrix is doubly stochastic when its entries are nonnegative and every row and column sums to one. The Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem says every such square matrix is a convex combination of permutation matrices. To see the decomposition, join a row to every column where it has positive mass. Any set of \(k\) rows sends out mass \(k\) and each neighboring column can receive at most one, so that set has at least \(k\) neighbors. Hall's matching theorem then supplies a selection of one positive entry per row and column. Subtract the smallest selected entry times the corresponding permutation matrix. Each residual row and column has the same sum. If that sum is positive, divide by it and repeat. At each subtraction at least one positive entry disappears, and restoring the normalization weights gives coefficients summing to one.

Now suppose \(A\) and \(B=A+F\) are both normal. Write \(\lambda_i,\mu_j\) for their eigenvalues with multiplicity and \(u_i,v_j\) for orthonormal eigenvectors. The weights \(p_{ij}=|u_i^*v_j|^2\) form a doubly stochastic matrix because expansion in either basis preserves squared norm. The Frobenius norm is \(\|M\|_F=(\sum_{ij}|m_{ij}|^2)^{1/2}\). Expand \(\operatorname{tr}((A-B)^*(A-B))\) in the two eigenbases. The cross trace is \(\operatorname{tr}(A^*B)=\sum_{ij}\overline{\lambda_i}\mu_jp_{ij}\). The remaining traces equal \(\sum_i|\lambda_i|^2\) and \(\sum_j|\mu_j|^2\). Because the weights have unit margins this gives

\[\|A-B\|_F^2=\sum_{i,j}p_{ij}|\lambda_i-\mu_j|^2 .\]

Decomposing \(p\) into permutation matrices expresses the right-hand side as an average of the total squared matching costs. Some permutation \(\sigma\) has cost at most that average:

\[\sum_i|\lambda_i-\mu_{\sigma(i)}|^2\le\|A-B\|_F^2 .\]

This is the Hoffman–Wielandt theorem. It controls the entire matching under a stronger structural hypothesis than one-sided normal Bauer–Fike. The spectral norm cannot generally replace the Frobenius norm in this sum bound. For \(B=A+\varepsilon I_n\) with \(A=0\), the two sides have sizes \(n|\varepsilon|^2\) and, upon that replacement, \(|\varepsilon|^2\). Hoffman and Wielandt, The Variation of the Spectrum of a Normal Matrix.

Our original controller is nonnormal. For a normal comparison, consider the same nominal rates in the diagonal model \(N=\operatorname{diag}(-2,-3,-8)\) and a symmetric reciprocal perturbation \(F=t(e_1e_2^T+e_2e_1^T)\). Then both \(N\) and \(N+F\) are Hermitian and the matching distance is bounded by \(\sqrt2|t|\). At \(t=0.02\), the two moving modes have shifts of magnitude \((\sqrt{1+4t^2}-1)/2\approx0.000400\). The two examples use different couplings and hypotheses, so the different shifts do not measure the benefit of any single numerical algorithm.

Exercises and solutions

A. Improve the fixed similarity

For \(S=\operatorname{diag}(s,1,1)\) with \(s>0\), find a choice that certifies decay for \(t=1\).

Solution. The two relevant row radii are \(4/s\) and \(s\). Their disks stay in the open left half-plane when \(s>2\) and \(s<3\). Taking \(s=2.5\) gives radii \(1.6\) and \(2.5\), both smaller than the magnitude of their diagonal centers. A suitable choice exists for every \(|t|<3/2\) since the conditions become \(s>2\) and \(s|t|<3\).

B. Check a collision without chasing individual disk labels

At \(t=0.1\) the fixed scaling \(s=8\) gives radii \(0.5,0.8,0\). How many eigenvalues lie in the union of the first two disks?

Solution. Their centers are one unit apart and the radii sum to \(1.3\), so the disks overlap. Their union stays disjoint from the disk at \(-8\), and the component theorem gives two eigenvalues in that union and one at \(-8\).

C. Make one mode insensitive to first order

Replace the feedback direction \(E\) by \(E_0=e_3e_1^T\). Compute the first derivative at the nominal eigenvalue \(-2\).

Solution. With the same \(x,y\) as before, \(E_0x=e_3\) is orthogonal to \(y\), so the derivative is zero. In fact the eigenvalues stay fixed here: moving the third coordinate to the first slot of the coordinate order makes the perturbed matrix triangular.

D. Distinguish defective sensitivity from numerical error

In the defective block take \(t=10^{-6}\). Compare the perturbation spectral norm and the eigenvalue displacement.

Solution. The perturbation is \(t e_2e_1^T\), with norm \(10^{-6}\). Each eigenvalue moves \(2\sqrt{10^{-6}}=0.002\). The shift follows from the exact characteristic polynomial and thus persists in exact arithmetic.

E. Show a two-mode matching can attain the bound

Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,0)\) and \(B=\operatorname{diag}(3,1)\). Compare both matching costs with the Frobenius bound.

Solution. Sorted matching costs \((2-3)^2+(0-1)^2=2\). The other matching costs \((2-1)^2+(0-3)^2=10\). The Frobenius norm squared is \(2\), so the best matching attains the bound.
