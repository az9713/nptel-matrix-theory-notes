Chapter 10 · Lectures 35–36

Product spectra and biorthogonal modes

L35 L36

An estimator stores three coordinates but updates them through only two latent signals. Computing modes in the smaller space saves work, and projecting states onto those modes requires left eigenvectors as well as right ones. A third coordinate can carry a zero mode that appears only in the larger matrix. The question is what information survives the change of space.

Let \(A\in\mathbb C^{m\times n}\) map latent signals to stored coordinates and \(B\in\mathbb C^{n\times m}\) return latent signals from stored coordinates. Then \(M=AB\) updates the stored state, and \(K=BA\) updates the latent state. The following dimensionless construction will keep the calculations explicit.

\[A=\begin{bmatrix}1&-4\\0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad B=\begin{bmatrix}0.8&3.2&0\\0&0.5&0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad K=\operatorname{diag}(0.8,0.5), \qquad M=\begin{bmatrix}0.8&1.2&0\\0&0.5&0\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.\]

When two updates share modes (L35 03:00)

For two individually diagonalizable matrices \(C,D\) on the same complex space, a common eigenbasis exists if and only if \(CD=DC\). The forward implication follows because diagonal matrices commute. For the reverse implication, \(C(Dv)=D(Cv)=\lambda Dv\) when \(Cv=\lambda v\), so \(D\) preserves each eigenspace of \(C\). Since \(D\) is diagonalizable, its eigenspace projectors are polynomials in \(D\): use the scalar Lagrange polynomials that take value one at one distinct eigenvalue and zero at the others. Their action on a \(D\)-eigenbasis proves they sum to the identity and land in the respective \(D\)-eigenspaces. These projectors preserve each \(C\)-eigenspace because it is invariant under \(D\). Decomposing each eigenspace of \(C\) by those projectors produces a common eigenbasis. The hypothesis that both are individually diagonalizable matters, because \(I_n\) commutes with a defective matrix and cannot supply its missing eigenvectors. In the latent estimator, \(K\) is diagonal with distinct entries, so any commuting second update has to preserve each of its two coordinate lines.

Moving eigenvectors between product spaces (L35 03:30)

Suppose \(Kv=\lambda v\) for \(v\ne0\) and \(\lambda\ne0\). Then \(Av\ne0\), since \(Av=0\) would give \(Kv=BAv=0\). Also \(MAv=AKv=\lambda Av\), so \(Av\) is a right eigenvector of \(M\). In reverse, \(B\) carries a right eigenvector of \(M\) to one of \(K\). On the corresponding eigenspaces, \((1/\lambda)B\) inverts \(A\) because \(BAv=\lambda v\), and \(ABw=\lambda w\) for any \(M\)-eigenvector \(w\) of eigenvalue \(\lambda\). Thus geometric multiplicities match at nonzero eigenvalues. The relation also holds on any transition time scale since \((AB)^kA=A(BA)^k\) by associativity, for integer \(k\ge0\).

A determinant identity gives the stronger statement about algebraic multiplicities. Introduce a scalar \(s\) and the block matrix

\[C(s)=\begin{bmatrix}I_m&A\\sB&I_n\end{bmatrix}.\]

Eliminate the lower-left block by subtracting \(sB\) times the top block row from the bottom block row. The resulting diagonal blocks are \(I_m\) and \(I_n-sBA\), so \(\det C(s)=\det(I_n-sBA)\). Alternatively subtract \(A\) times the bottom block row from the top block row. The resulting block triangular matrix has determinant \(\det(I_m-sAB)\). Both operations multiply by block triangular matrices of determinant one. For \(\lambda\ne0\), substitute \(s=1/\lambda\) and restore powers of \(\lambda\) in each determinant. When \(m\ge n\), polynomial equality then gives

\[p_M(\lambda)=\lambda^{m-n}p_K(\lambda)\]

for all \(\lambda\), where \(p_M(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I_m-M)\) and \(p_K(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I_n-K)\). When \(n>m\), interchange the roles of the products. This determines the extra algebraic multiplicity at zero. The eigenvector transfer proof involves dividing by \(\lambda\), and the map can collapse the zero eigenspace. Even square \(A\) and \(B\) can have different shifted ranks at zero, as an exercise shows.

In the estimator, multiplying the coordinate vectors of \(K\) by \(A\) gives \(v_1=(1,0,0)^T\) and \(v_2=(-4,1,0)^T\). The product matrix also kills \(v_3=(0,0,1)^T\). For a large stored space, the spectrum of \(K\) is a smaller eigenproblem, and the product structure gives the missing algebraic zero count. Any analysis of the space killed by \(M\) needs more information than this count.

Which rows extract the mode amplitudes? (L36 10:38)

A left eigenvector is represented by a nonzero column \(u\) whose adjoint row \(u^*\) satisfies \(u^*M=\lambda u^*\). Here \(u^*\) is the conjugate transpose. Equivalently \(M^*u=\overline\lambda u\), so conjugation matters for nonreal eigenvalues. This is the convention used by LAPACK. LAPACK Users’ Guide, Nonsymmetric Eigenproblems

If \(Mv_j=\lambda_j v_j\) and \(u_i^*M=\lambda_i u_i^*\), evaluate \(u_i^*Mv_j\) by applying either eigenvector equation. The equality becomes \((\lambda_i-\lambda_j)u_i^*v_j=0\). Distinct eigenvalues therefore give \(u_i^*v_j=0\). Suppose now that \(M\) has an invertible right eigenvector matrix \(V=[v_1\ \cdots\ v_m]\) and diagonal eigenvalue matrix \(\Lambda\). Since \(MV=V\Lambda\), we have \(V^{-1}M=\Lambda V^{-1}\). Define \(u_i^*\) as row \(i\) of \(V^{-1}\). These rows give the left eigenvectors with the normalization \(u_i^*v_j=\delta_{ij}\), where \(\delta_{ij}\) is one for equal indices and zero otherwise. This is biorthogonality. Within a repeated eigenspace, the inverse-basis construction selects matching dual directions. Arbitrary left and right eigenvector bases with the same order need not pair this way.

Define the spectral projector onto the \(i\)th chosen direction by \(P_i=v_i u_i^*\). The scalar \(u_i^*x\) gives its coefficient in a state \(x\). The identity \(u_i^*v_j=\delta_{ij}\) gives \(P_iP_j=\delta_{ij}P_i\) and \(\sum_iP_i=I_m\). Hence for \(k\ge1\),

\[M^k x=\sum_i\lambda_i^kP_i x.\]

For \(M\) above, the first two rows of \(V^{-1}\) are \((1,4,0)\) and \((0,1,0)\). From \(x=(0,1,0)^T\) the initial coefficient of the \(0.8\) mode is four, and the coefficient of the \(0.5\) mode is one. At step \(k\) the first coordinate becomes \(4(0.8^k-0.5^k)\). The Euclidean inner product of \(v_1\) with \(x\) is zero and would miss that contribution. The spectral projectors here are oblique, meaning their complementary subspaces need not be perpendicular. A single index within a repeated eigenspace can depend on the chosen basis, while summing its projectors over that eigenvalue yields the projector onto the whole eigenspace along the other eigenspaces.

First-order sensitivity of the mode value

Consider a simple eigenvalue \(\lambda\), right eigenvector \(v\) and dual row \(u^*\) with \(u^*v=1\). Such scaling is possible for a simple eigenvalue. If every left eigenvector row at \(\lambda\) killed \(v\), then \(v\) would lie in \(\mathcal R(M-\lambda I_m)\), the orthogonal complement of the kernel of \((M-\lambda I_m)^*\). There would then be a vector \(w\) with \((M-\lambda I_m)w=v\). On the invariant span of \(v,w\), the characteristic polynomial is \((t-\lambda)^2\), contradicting simplicity. Let a perturbation \(M+hF\) depend on a real scalar \(h\) and a fixed matrix \(F\). A simple eigenvalue has a locally differentiable eigenvalue and eigenvector branch. One justification uses the implicit function theorem on \((M+hF)v(h)=\lambda(h)v(h)\) with \(u^*v(h)=1\). In its derivative with respect to \((v,\lambda)\), the homogeneous equations at \(h=0\) give \((M-\lambda I_m)\dot v=\dot\lambda v\) and \(u^*\dot v=0\). Multiplication by \(u^*\) gives \(\dot\lambda=0\), forcing \(\dot v\) into the one-dimensional eigenspace and then to zero. This square derivative is invertible.

Differentiate the eigenvector equation at zero and multiply by \(u^*\). The terms containing \(v'(0)\) cancel, leaving

\[\lambda'(0)=u^* F v, \qquad |\lambda'(0)|\le\|u\|_2\|F\|_2\|v\|_2,\]

where the matrix \(2\)-norm is induced by the Euclidean norm. The coefficient \(\|u\|_2\|v\|_2\) is \(\|vu^*\|_2\), since Cauchy–Schwarz gives an upper bound and taking the unit vector \(u/\|u\|_2\) attains it. For the \(0.8\) mode, it is \(\sqrt{17}\). In particular, choose \(F\) with a one in entry \((2,1)\) and zeros elsewhere. The response coefficient is four, because \(Fv_1=(0,1,0)^T\) and \(u_1^*Fv_1=4\). This first-order bound allows any \(F\) in the full matrix space. Perturbations constrained to factored updates have additional structure, since first derivatives in \(A\) and \(B\) enter as \((\Delta A)B+A(\Delta B)\).

Laboratory

Run ch10.py with Python and NumPy. It creates the two products, checks their spectra and left-right pairings, and compares the analytic sensitivity with a one-sided finite difference. A failed check raises an assertion. The run printed derivative \(4.000000\) and projector norm \(4.123106\). The finite-difference check uses a fixed step \(10^{-7}\) and double precision on this example. It does not give a perturbation guarantee for larger changes or clustered eigenvalues.

Exercises and solutions

  1. Construct square \(A,B\) such that \(AB\) and \(BA\) have different nullities.
    Solution. Take \(A=e_1e_1^T\) and \(B=e_1e_2^T\) in two dimensions, with \(e_i\) the coordinate vectors. Then \(AB=B\) has rank one, whereas \(BA=0\) because \(e_2^Te_1=0\). Both products have characteristic polynomial \(t^2\).

  2. Let \(u^*K=\lambda u^*\) for \(\lambda\ne0\). Transfer this left eigenvector to \(M\).
    Solution. Compute \((u^*B)M=u^*BAB=\lambda u^*B\). The row \(u^*B\) cannot vanish, since then \(u^*BA=0\) would contradict \(\lambda u^*\ne0\).

  3. In the estimator, find a norm-one \(F\) that attains the derivative magnitude bound for the first mode.
    Solution. Take \(F=u_1v_1^*/(\|u_1\|_2\|v_1\|_2)\). It maps the unit vector in the \(v_1\) direction to the unit vector in the \(u_1\) direction and has induced norm one. Substitution gives \(u_1^*Fv_1=\sqrt{17}\).

  4. Show that a real \(2\times2\) matrix with sole eigenvalue one can prevent biorthogonal normalization for an eigenvector.
    Solution. Use \(M=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\). A right eigenvector has zero second component, whereas a left eigenvector row has zero first component. Their pairing vanishes under every scaling. The inverse-basis construction requires a complete eigenbasis, which this matrix lacks.

Reducing to \(BA\) recovers the nonzero spectral values and their eigenspaces through the factors. Computing modal amplitudes and first-order changes also requires the dual geometry of those eigenspaces.
