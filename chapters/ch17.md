# Interlacing and majorization

An actuator team knows the stiffness spectrum of a two-coordinate assembly and wants to add a third coordinate without changing the energy of displacements confined to the original two. The customer names three target modal stiffnesses and also wants to know what can be inferred from tests on isolated coordinates. Adding one coordinate and rotating the experimental basis have different mathematical effects.

The original model has stiffnesses $6$ and $2$. Work in its orthonormal modal coordinates, where its stiffness matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(6,2)$. All values below use normalized masses and dimensionless units. The target extended spectrum is $(8,4,1)$, written in descending order.

## Compression and interlacing

[L65 01:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fx7AGYFP4Mo&t=61s) · [L67 17:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5YWOa3AnZxM&t=1021s) · [L67 32:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5YWOa3AnZxM&t=1920s)

If $H$ is Hermitian of order $N$, choose an $N$-by-$m$ matrix $Q$ with orthonormal columns. Its **compression** to the column space $S$ of $Q$ is $C=Q^*HQ$. For coordinates $y$ of a nonzero vector in this space, $R_C(y)=R_H(Qy)$ since $\|Qy\|_2=\|y\|_2$. Removing the same set of row and column indices gives a principal submatrix, the special case in which $Q$ consists of coordinate vectors.

Order both spectra in descending order. Then
$$
\lambda_i(H)\ge\lambda_i(C)\ge\lambda_{i+N-m}(H),\qquad 1\le i\le m.
$$
The max–min formula from [Courant–Fischer](ch16.html) computes $\lambda_i(C)$ by searching only $i$-dimensional subspaces of $S$. Enlarging the search to all such subspaces of the ambient space raises or preserves the optimum. The min–max formula searches only $(m-i+1)$-dimensional subspaces of $S$. Enlarging this search lowers or preserves the optimum. In the ambient space this dimension addresses index $N-(m-i+1)+1=i+N-m$, which proves the lower bound.

For a border from order $m$ to order $m+1$, the extended eigenvalues $\mu_i$ and the original eigenvalues $\alpha_i$ must satisfy $\mu_i\ge\alpha_i\ge\mu_{i+1}$. The desired order-three spectrum satisfies $8\ge6\ge4\ge2\ge1$. A requested spectrum $(8,7,1)$ fails because the largest old stiffness $6$ is below the second requested stiffness $7$. No choice of couplings and new-coordinate stiffness can meet that target while keeping the original block.

For an additive rank-one update the ambient dimension stays fixed. In [the preceding chapter](ch16.html) the Weyl and positive semidefinite bounds gave $\lambda_i(A+\tau zz^*)\ge\lambda_i(A)\ge\lambda_{i+1}(A+\tau zz^*)$ for $i<n$ and $\tau>0$, with monotonicity also at $i=n$. Here interlacing restricts how far the old modes move. Under bordering it restricts values that the larger model can have around a compressed spectrum.

## Constructing an extension from target eigenvalues

[L66 02:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YJWyKLguyRs&t=153s) · [L67 09:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5YWOa3AnZxM&t=570s) · [L67 13:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5YWOa3AnZxM&t=781s)

Let $\alpha_1\ge\cdots\ge\alpha_m$ and $\mu_1\ge\cdots\ge\mu_{m+1}$ interlace. We seek
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}D&z\\z^T&b\end{pmatrix},\qquad
D=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m),\quad z\in\mathbb R^m,\quad b\in\mathbb R.
$$
Trace forces $b=\sum_i\mu_i-\sum_i\alpha_i$. Write $p(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{m+1}(t-\mu_i)$ and $q(t)=\prod_{i=1}^m(t-\alpha_i)$. For $t\notin\{\alpha_i\}$, eliminate the lower-left block of $tI_{m+1}-M$ using its invertible upper-left block. Its determinant then becomes
$$
q(t)\left(t-b-\sum_{j=1}^m\frac{z_j^2}{t-\alpha_j}\right).
$$
Assume first that the $\alpha_i$ are distinct. The rational function $p(t)/q(t)$ has polynomial part $t-b$ and simple-pole coefficients $p(\alpha_j)/q'(\alpha_j)$, obtained by multiplying by $t-\alpha_j$ and evaluating the limit. Therefore choose $z_j^2=-p(\alpha_j)/q'(\alpha_j)$. Under strict interlacing, the numerator product has $j$ negative factors and the denominator has $j-1$, so the negative ratio is positive. A weak equality may instead give a zero numerator, in which case that coordinate can have zero coupling. The determinant formula equals $p$ off the poles and hence everywhere, as both characteristic polynomials agree on infinitely many arguments.

If a value $\theta$ occurs $r$ times among the old eigenvalues, the $r-1$ targets between consecutive copies are forced to equal $\theta$. Remove those $r-1$ old and target copies, retaining one old copy. The reduced lists still interlace. Construct the extension there and restore the removed old axes with zero coupling. These axes contribute the removed target multiplicities. This handles repeated values without dividing by a vanishing derivative.

For $D=\operatorname{diag}(6,2)$ and target $(8,4,1)$, the trace condition gives $b=5$. The formula gives $z_1^2=5$ and $z_2^2=3$. The real symmetric extension therefore exists with $z=(\sqrt5,\sqrt3)^T$. The target eigenvalues are all positive, so they certify positive energy in every nonzero extended displacement. Interlacing alone certifies feasibility in the class of real symmetric extensions. Physical constraints on sign, connectivity or allowable coupling magnitudes may restrict the available designs further.

## Diagonals versus spectra

[L68 00:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lxS0N8jS1HM&t=31s) · [L68 06:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lxS0N8jS1HM&t=373s)

For a real vector $x$ let $x^\downarrow$ denote its descending rearrangement. Write $x\prec y$ when $x$ is **majorized by** $y$, meaning
$$
\sum_{i=1}^k x_i^\downarrow\le \sum_{i=1}^k y_i^\downarrow
\quad(1\le k<n),\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^n x_i=\sum_{i=1}^n y_i.
$$
The equivalent test with ascending order compares sums of the smallest coordinates with the opposite inequality. These two tests use the same trace equality.

If $d$ is the diagonal of a Hermitian $H$, then $d\prec \lambda(H)$. To see this, let $C$ be the principal submatrix retained by selecting the $k$ largest diagonal entries. Its trace is their sum, and by interlacing its $i$th largest eigenvalue is at most $\lambda_i(H)$ for $1\le i\le k$. Summing proves the required partial-sum inequality. When $k=n$ we have trace equality.

The constructed extension has sorted diagonal $(6,5,2)$ and spectrum $(8,4,1)$. Both total $13$. The top-one and top-two pairs are $6\le8$ and $11\le12$. In this three-coordinate case both proper partial sums follow from the individual extremal-coordinate bounds and trace equality. For an independent intermediate-sum obstruction, compare spectrum $(8,4,3,1)$ and diagonal $(7,6,2,1)$. Each entry is in $[1,8]$ and both total $16$, but $7+6>8+4$.

A subspace form of the same argument gives a bound on eigenvalues of a sum. For any Hermitian $H$ and any matrix $Q$ with $k$ orthonormal columns, interlacing gives $\operatorname{tr}(Q^*HQ)\le \sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_i(H)$. Choosing the top $k$ eigenvectors gives equality. For same-order Hermitian matrices $A,B$, take $Q$ to be the maximizing frame for $H=A+B$. The sum of the two traces for $A$ and $B$ is bounded by the sum of their respective largest-$k$ spectral totals. Hence $\lambda(A+B)\prec\lambda(A)+\lambda(B)$, where the vectors on the right are sorted before addition. At $k=n$ equality follows by trace linearity. The inequality allows cancellation when different matrices concentrate their largest stiffnesses in different coordinate directions.

## The Schur–Horn converse

[L68 19:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lxS0N8jS1HM&t=1140s)

For real lists $d,\alpha$ with $d\prec\alpha$, there exists a real symmetric matrix with diagonal $d$ and eigenvalues $\alpha$. The following rotation argument constructs its basis. Sort both lists descending and work with the operator $\operatorname{diag}(\alpha)$. Its largest and smallest eigenvalues bound $d_1$ by majorization and the fact that $d_1$ is at least the average. If the $\alpha_i$ are all equal, both lists are constant. Otherwise choose neighboring entries $\alpha_j\ge d_1\ge\alpha_{j+1}$. A real unit vector in their coordinate plane can realize $d_1$ by taking squared components $s,1-s$ such that $s\alpha_j+(1-s)\alpha_{j+1}=d_1$. The perpendicular unit vector in the same plane has energy $b=\alpha_j+\alpha_{j+1}-d_1$. The compression after fixing the first basis vector has descending spectrum
$$
(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{j-1},b,\alpha_{j+2},\ldots,\alpha_n).
$$
Indeed, the other old axes stay diagonal and $b$ lies between the two removed entries.

The new list majorizes $(d_2,\ldots,d_n)$. For a prefix of length $k<j$, its entries are each at least $d_1$, so their sum is at least the desired prefix sum. For $k\ge j$, the sum is the old prefix of length $k+1$ minus $d_1$, so the original inequalities apply and the new total matches. Induction constructs an orthonormal basis of the remaining subspace with the desired diagonal. The full basis gives a real orthogonal similarity, preserving the original spectrum. A permutation restores the specified order of the diagonal. This is the finite real construction behind the sufficiency direction. [Horn's original paper](https://doi.org/10.2307/2372705).

## Exercises and solutions

### A. Identify a new-coordinate stiffness without solving the inverse problem

For the original actuator spectrum $(6,2)$ and extension target $(9,3,1)$, find $b$ and decide whether the target is feasible over real symmetric matrices.

**Solution.** The upper and lower bounds read $9\ge6\ge3\ge2\ge1$, so the border converse applies. The trace difference is $13-8=5$, hence $b=5$.

### B. Deal with a shared endpoint

For the old spectrum $(6,2)$ and target $(8,6,1)$, compute the two squared coupling entries.

**Solution.** The product $p(6)=0$ forces $z_1=0$. At $2$, $p(2)=(-6)(-4)(1)=24$ and $q'(2)=-4$, giving $z_2^2=6$. The new diagonal is $b=7$. The first old coordinate remains an eigenvector at $6$ while the remaining block has trace $9$ and determinant $8$, giving eigenvalues $8,1$.

### C. Delete two coordinates at once

An order-four Hermitian matrix has spectrum $(9,6,4,1)$. What bounds follow for the two eigenvalues of an order-two principal submatrix?

**Solution.** With $N-m=2$, the largest compression eigenvalue is in $[4,9]$ and the smallest is in $[1,6]$. Their sorted order is a further condition. A proposed pair $(3,2)$ violates the first bound.

### D. Design a flat diagonal by rotation

Realize diagonal $(4,4)$ and eigenvalues $(6,2)$ in a real symmetric matrix.

**Solution.** Use basis vectors $(1,1)^T/\sqrt2$ and $(1,-1)^T/\sqrt2$ for the diagonal operator. Both quotients are $4$ and their cross term is $(6-2)/2=2$. This yields $\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$, whose spectrum is indeed $(6,2)$.

### E. Strict majorization for a sum

Take $A=\operatorname{diag}(6,2)$ and $B=\operatorname{diag}(2,6)$. Compare $\lambda(A+B)$ with the componentwise sum of their ordered spectra.

**Solution.** The ordered sum is $(12,4)$ whereas the actual spectrum is $(8,8)$. At the first prefix $8<12$ and both lists total $16$. Each matrix has a different unique maximal eigendirection, so a one-dimensional subspace cannot simultaneously attain both separate trace maxima.
