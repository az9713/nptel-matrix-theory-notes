Chapter 14 · Lectures 52–56

Elimination, pivoting, and Cholesky factorization

L52 L53 L54 L55 L56

A calibration rig fits unknown gains by solving the same coefficient system for many measurements. One sensor responds weakly to the first gain, so eliminating that gain from its equation first can erase information in the arithmetic. Swapping two equations may fix that failure. A second stage uses a positive definite weighting matrix, where its symmetry and positivity justify a different factorization.

The original examples use dimensionless coefficients. Start with \(Ax=b\), where \(A\) is an invertible square matrix of order \(n\), \(b\) is a vector of observations, and \(x\) is the unknown. The first example will use

\[A_\epsilon=\begin{bmatrix}\epsilon&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad b=(1+\epsilon,2)^T,\qquad 0<\epsilon<1.\]

The exact solution is \((1,1)^T\). The determinant is \(\epsilon-1\), so a small first diagonal coefficient does not make this example nearly singular as \(\epsilon\) tends to zero.

Gaussian elimination and stored multipliers (L52 00:45, L53 00:30)

An active matrix at step \(k\) is the block of remaining equations and variables with indices \(k,\ldots,n\). The pivot is the leading entry of that block. To eliminate the first column below a nonzero pivot \(\alpha\), write the current block as

\[\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&r\\c&C\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\c/\alpha&I\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}\alpha&r\\0&C-(c/\alpha)r\end{bmatrix},\]

where \(r\) is a row and \(c\) a column. The lower-left entries \(c/\alpha\) store the multipliers used to subtract pivot-row contributions. The new active block \(C-(c/\alpha)r\) is the Schur complement of \(\alpha\). It gives the remaining coefficients after eliminating the first variable. Recursive use of the identity gives \(A=LU\) if every pivot is nonzero, with \(L\) unit lower triangular and \(U\) upper triangular. Embedding each smaller lower triangular factor into the full matrix accumulates the multipliers, and \(U\) holds the retained pivot rows.

For invertible \(A\), existence of this unpivoted factorization is equivalent to all leading principal minors being nonzero. A leading principal minor of order \(k\) is the determinant of the submatrix in the first \(k\) rows and columns. If the factorization exists, its leading block is the product of the leading blocks of \(L\) and \(U\), so its determinant is the product of the first \(k\) pivots. In reverse, after successfully eliminating the first \(k-1\) pivots, apply the determinant identity to the leading block through order \(k\). The current candidate pivot times the previous product equals that minor. Its nonzero value permits elimination to continue.

With \(A_\epsilon\), the first multiplier without a swap is \(1/\epsilon\), and the second pivot is \(1-1/\epsilon\). Exact arithmetic gives \(x_2=(2-(1+\epsilon)/\epsilon)/(1-1/\epsilon)=1\), then \(x_1=(1+\epsilon-x_2)/\epsilon=1\). In single precision at \(\epsilon=10^{-8}\), \(1+\epsilon\) rounds to one. Even with that rounded data, a solve can approximate the true solution because the exact solution of the rounded equations is close to \((1,1)^T\). The unpivoted algorithm below first obtains \(x_2=1\) in working precision, then forms \(1-x_2=0\) and divides by the small pivot, losing the first component.

Partial pivoting and solving the permuted system (L54 08:32, L55 00:34)

Partial pivoting selects a row whose active-column entry has largest modulus and swaps it into the first position of the active block. In exact arithmetic at any step on an invertible matrix, that largest modulus is nonzero. After previous steps the system is block upper triangular with an invertible leading triangular block, so its active block must be invertible too. A zero active column would contradict that conclusion. Dividing each active-column entry by the selected pivot gives multipliers of modulus at most one. This constrains each elementary elimination. The successive active coefficients may still grow.

Let \(P\) be the permutation matrix that applies the cumulative row swaps, so \(PA=LU\). At a later swap, the previously stored multipliers in the two swapped rows must also swap. Algebraically, moving a row permutation through an earlier elimination conjugates that elimination matrix by the permutation. This relocates its stored multipliers with the correct rows. After factorization the solution uses \(L y=Pb\) by forward substitution and \(U x=y\) by backward substitution. Applying the same permutation to the data is necessary because the swaps change the order of equations. LAPACK's guide writes \(A=P_{\mathrm{doc}}LU\), with \(P_{\mathrm{doc}}=P^{-1}=P^T\) under our convention. LAPACK Users’ Guide, Linear Equations

For the calibration example, the pivot is one after swapping. The multiplier becomes \(\epsilon\) and the active second equation has coefficient \(1-\epsilon\). With exact data its right-hand side is \(1+\epsilon-2\epsilon=1-\epsilon\). Thus \(x_2=1\) and the pivot row gives \(x_1=2-x_2=1\). The final subtraction uses terms of moderate size. After a dense factorization, the two triangular solves take on the order of \(n^2\) scalar operations per right-hand side, since each one sums progressively longer rows. Summing the active rank-one update sizes \((n-k)^2\) over elimination steps gives work on the order of \(n^3\) for the original factorization. Reusing the factors is useful if the coefficient matrix stays fixed.

L54 10:04 covers complete pivoting, which extends the choice of largest modulus to the entire active block. This may require column swaps, which permute the unknowns while row swaps permute equations. Let \(\Pi\) denote the accumulated column permutation so that \(PA\Pi=LU\). Set \(z=\Pi^Tx\), solve \(Ly=Pb\) and \(Uz=y\), then recover \(x=\Pi z\). Omitting the final permutation can yield correctly computed values assigned to the wrong gains. Searching the active block needs a quadratic number of comparisons per step in its dimension, whereas partial pivoting scans one column. The computational decision depends on the cost of those searches and the coefficient behavior.

LDM decomposition and Hermitian structure (L55 10:16)

If \(A=LU\) with an invertible \(U\), put \(D=\operatorname{diag}(u_{11},\ldots,u_{nn})\) and \(R=D^{-1}U\). Then \(A=LDR\) with \(L\) unit lower triangular, \(R\) unit upper triangular, and \(D\) diagonal with nonzero entries. In the real case \(R\) also has the form \(M^T\) for a unit lower triangular \(M\), giving the notation \(LDM^T\). Uniqueness follows by comparing two such decompositions. If \(LDR=\widetilde L\widetilde D\widetilde R\), then \(\widetilde L^{-1}L=\widetilde D\widetilde R R^{-1}D^{-1}\). The left side is unit lower triangular and the right side upper triangular, so both equal the identity. Then comparison of upper diagonals gives \(D=\widetilde D\) and the unit upper factors agree.

For a Hermitian \(A\) with \(A=A^*\), the adjoint of \(LDR\) is \(R^*D^*L^*\), which is another decomposition of the same kind. Uniqueness gives \(R=L^*\) and \(D^*=D\). This yields \(A=LDL^*\) under the nonzero-pivot hypothesis. The identity does not automatically extend to a row-pivoted Hermitian matrix \(PA\) because row swaps alone may break Hermitian symmetry. In a symmetric indefinite solve one can instead permute rows and columns together, and block pivots may be needed. The example \(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\) has two zero diagonal entries but is itself invertible, so no simultaneous swap produces a nonzero scalar first diagonal pivot. The LAPACK indefinite factorization allows \(2\times2\) diagonal blocks. LAPACK Users’ Guide, Linear Equations

Cholesky from positive Schur complements (L56 06:28)

A Hermitian matrix \(H\) is positive definite if \(x^*Hx>0\) for every nonzero vector \(x\). Let

\[H=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&c^*\\c&C\end{bmatrix}.\]

Then \(\alpha>0\) by testing the first coordinate. For any nonzero column \(y\) of the remaining dimension, choose the vector \(x=(-c^*y/\alpha,y)^T\). Direct multiplication gives \(x^*Hx=y^*(C-cc^*/\alpha)y\). Therefore the Schur complement is positive definite. Recursion proves every pivot is positive. This produces \(H=LDL^*\) with \(D\) positive diagonal, and setting \(G=LD^{1/2}\) gives the Cholesky factorization \(H=GG^*\) with \(G\) lower triangular and positive real diagonal. Conversely an invertible lower triangular \(G\) produces a positive definite \(GG^*\), because \(x^*GG^*x=\|G^*x\|_2^2>0\) for \(x\ne0\). The Cholesky factor under this diagonal convention is unique: divide each column by its diagonal to obtain the unit lower triangular factor and let the squared diagonals form \(D\), then use uniqueness of \(LDL^*\).

For the rig's weighting matrix \(H=\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\), the first multiplier is \(2/4=0.5\) and the remaining pivot is \(3-2^2/4=2\). Completing the square gives \(x^*Hx=4|x_1+x_2/2|^2+2|x_2|^2\), certifying positive definiteness. Then

\[G=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\1&\sqrt2\end{bmatrix}.\]

The factor gives a triangular solve for \(Hx=b\) and converts the quadratic energy to the squared Euclidean norm of \(G^*x\). The positive-definiteness proof concerns exact arithmetic. At very small pivots, rounding can affect whether the computed Schur complement is positive. In numerical work, a factorization failure is evidence to inspect scaling and the input rather than a sign test for the exact physical model.

Exercises and solutions

  1. Find an invertible \(A\) with no unpivoted \(LU\) form under the conventions above.
    Solution. The swap matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\) has determinant \(-1\) but its first leading minor is zero. An invertible unpivoted \(LU\) needs a nonzero first minor, so it fails. Applying the same swap to its rows gives the identity, which has a trivial factorization.

  2. Explain why partial pivoting cannot prevent every large entry in \(U\).
    Solution. A multiplier of modulus at most one limits propagation per update, but it does not prevent contributions of opposite sign from adding when subtracted. For \(\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\) choosing the first row in the tie gives second pivot \(1-1(-1)=2\), twice the largest initial magnitude.

  3. Use the factors of \(H\) to solve \(Hx=(6,5)^T\).
    Solution. With \(G\) displayed above, solving \(Gy=(6,5)^T\) gives \(y_1=3\) and \(y_2=\sqrt2\). Then \(G^*x=y\) gives \(x_2=1\) and \(2x_1+1=3\), so \(x=(1,1)^T\).

  4. Find the positivity threshold for the symmetric matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&s\end{bmatrix}\) with real parameter \(s\).
    Solution. The Schur complement is \(s-1\). Completing the square yields \(4|x_1+x_2/2|^2+(s-1)|x_2|^2\). For \(s>1\) every nonzero vector has positive energy. At \(s=1\), the vector \((-1/2,1)^T\) has zero energy and the final pivot vanishes. For \(s<1\) that vector has negative energy.

Elimination stores each equation update for reuse. Pivoting adjusts the equation order when the next divisor is weak, and positive definiteness makes the Schur complements suitable for Cholesky without row swaps.
