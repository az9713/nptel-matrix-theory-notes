# Hermitian matrices and Rayleigh–Ritz

A vibration model for two coupled actuators must pass a minimum stiffness requirement in every unit displacement direction. Measuring the energy of the two individual actuator commands misses a third possibility: a collective displacement that costs less than either command on its own.

Use dimensionless coordinates and normalized masses. For a real displacement $x=(x_1,x_2)^T$, the modeled energy is
$$
f(x)=\tfrac12\bigl(2x_1^2+4x_2^2+(x_1-x_2)^2\bigr).
$$
The release criterion is that $2f(x)$ exceed $3$ for every unit displacement. The test is about the model's worst direction. The model omits damping and assumes the spring law remains linear over the displacements of interest.

## How symmetric matrices arise

[L57 00:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OWBlT_w0awY&t=51s) · [L57 07:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OWBlT_w0awY&t=422s)

For a twice continuously differentiable real energy function, the Hessian has entries $h_{ij}=\partial^2 f/\partial x_i\partial x_j$. Equality of mixed partial derivatives gives $H=H^T$. Here the Hessian is constant and $2f(x)=x^THx$ with
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&5\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The coupling spring contributes the symmetric term $(e_1-e_2)(e_1-e_2)^T$, where $e_j$ is coordinate vector $j$. In a network, the adjacency matrix of an undirected graph is symmetric because an edge between $i$ and $j$ is a relation in both directions. A weighted spring network yields another symmetric matrix by summing one such coupling term per edge.

For complex vectors the corresponding definition uses the adjoint $H^*=\overline H^T$. A matrix with $H^*=H$ is **Hermitian**. Each diagonal entry is real. In the quadratic form $x^*Hx$, off-diagonal entries pair as conjugates, so their sum is real too. Conversely, if $x^*Ax$ is real for every complex $x$, then $x^*(A-A^*)x=0$. Writing $M=A-A^*$, testing $e_i$ gives $m_{ii}=0$. Testing $e_i+e_j$ gives $m_{ij}+m_{ji}=0$ and testing $e_i+i e_j$ gives $i(m_{ij}-m_{ji})=0$. Thus $A=A^*$. The complex tests matter. Over the reals, the form of any real matrix equals the form of its symmetric part because the skew-symmetric part contributes zero.

## An orthonormal basis of modes

[L58 01:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=izcaXw_WAtw&t=60s) · [L58 28:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=izcaXw_WAtw&t=1689s)

Every square complex $A$ decomposes as $(A+A^*)/2+(A-A^*)/2$, a Hermitian part and a skew-Hermitian part (a matrix $K$ with $K^*=-K$). The adjoint reverses product order. Therefore $AB$ is Hermitian for Hermitian $A,B$ precisely when $AB=BA$. For any rectangular matrix $C$, $C^*C$ is Hermitian with $x^*C^*Cx=\|Cx\|_2^2\ge 0$, where $\|x\|_2=\sqrt{x^*x}$.

**Spectral theorem.** A complex Hermitian matrix admits a factorization $H=U\Lambda U^*$ with $U^*U=I_n$ and $\Lambda$ real diagonal. A real symmetric matrix admits such a factorization with real $U$.

For a nonzero eigenvector $u$ with $Hu=\lambda u$, we have $\lambda=(u^*Hu)/(u^*u)\in\mathbb R$. Existence of a complex eigenvalue follows from the characteristic polynomial. Normalize $u$ to unit length. For any $v$ perpendicular to $u$, $u^*Hv=(Hu)^*v=\lambda u^*v=0$, so its orthogonal complement is invariant. The restriction remains Hermitian. Induction on the dimension supplies an orthonormal eigenbasis, including when an eigenvalue repeats. In the real symmetric case, since the eigenvalue is real, the real or imaginary part of a complex eigenvector supplies a nonzero real eigenvector. The same induction works in the real orthogonal complement. Conversely a factorization of the stated form gives $H^*=H$ by taking adjoints.

A matrix is **normal** if $A^*A=AA^*$. Every Hermitian matrix is normal. Conversely, by the unitary diagonalization theorem for normal matrices, a normal matrix with real eigenvalues has a unitary factorization with a real diagonal and is Hermitian. Reality of the spectrum alone is insufficient: $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ has real eigenvalues $1,2$ and unequal off-diagonal entries.

The actuator eigenvalues solve $(3-\lambda)(5-\lambda)-1=0$, giving $4\pm\sqrt2$. For the smaller eigenvalue the equation in the first row gives $x_2=(\sqrt2-1)x_1$. Both actuators move in the same direction while the softer actuator moves farther. The two coordinate experiments report $3$ and $5$. The soft mode gives approximately $2.586$, so the model fails the requirement.

## Rayleigh–Ritz and a residual check

[L59 02:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f8CQBav1R6E&t=120s)

For nonzero $x$ define the Rayleigh quotient $R_H(x)=x^*Hx/(x^*x)$. Write eigenvalues in **descending** order $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_n$, reversing the lecture indexing. With $c=U^*x$, spectral coordinates give
$$
R_H(x)=\sum_{j=1}^n\lambda_j\frac{|c_j|^2}{\sum_i |c_i|^2}.
$$
The nonnegative weights sum to one, so $R_H(x)$ lies between $\lambda_n$ and $\lambda_1$. Either endpoint is attained exactly by the nonzero vectors in the corresponding eigenspace, since all coefficients with a different eigenvalue must vanish at equality. In particular, a unit vector with $R_H(x)\le 3$ refutes the strict actuator requirement. The unit direction of $(1,\tfrac12)^T$ has quotient $2.6$.

The smallest and largest eigenvalues bound the measured quotient, with equality allowed. Approximate modes need an additional test. For any nonzero $x$ and real $\rho$, the residual $r=Hx-\rho x$ satisfies
$$
\frac{\|r\|_2^2}{\|x\|_2^2}
=\frac{\sum_j(\lambda_j-\rho)^2|c_j|^2}{\sum_j|c_j|^2}
\ge \min_j|\lambda_j-\rho|^2 .
$$
For the trial vector above and $\rho=2.6$, the relative residual $\|r\|_2/\|x\|_2$ is $0.2$. It certifies an eigenvalue within $0.2$ of the observed quotient. If we know all other eigenvalues are at distance at least $g>0$ from $\rho$, the same sum bounds the squared fraction of $x$ in their eigenspaces by $\|r\|_2^2/(g^2\|x\|_2^2)$. A tight cluster can leave the direction of any one eigenvector poorly determined even when the residual is small.

## Restricting the search

[L60 28:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AmDxlz8KMas&t=1680s)

[L60 12:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AmDxlz8KMas&t=763s)

More generally, maximizing over $x\perp u_1,\ldots,u_{k-1}$ gives $\lambda_k$, attained by $u_k$. Minimizing over $x\perp u_{k+1},\ldots,u_n$ also gives $\lambda_k$. These constrained problems assume access to the indicated modes. An alternative chooses the constraint itself as part of the optimization.

For $n\geq 2$, removing known modes restricts the weights in the Rayleigh quotient. Maximizing over $x\perp u_1$ gives $\lambda_2$ because the first weight is zero and $u_2$ attains the largest remaining value. To see why a clever alternate constraint cannot reduce this maximum further, take any hyperplane $W$ of allowable vectors. The intersection of $W$ with $\operatorname{span}(u_1,u_2)$ has dimension at least one. For a nonzero vector in that intersection, the quotient is at least $\lambda_2$. Therefore the minimum, over all hyperplanes, of the maximum quotient is $\lambda_2$. This conclusion remains true when the top eigenvalue has multiplicity greater than one. In the actuator model a hyperplane is a line, and the best such line is the soft mode. The argument generalizes to every index in [Courant–Fischer](ch16.html).

## Exercises and solutions

### A. Recover the coupled stiffness

Derive the Hessian from the three spring contributions. Then find the worst stiffness when the coupling spring is removed.

**Solution.** The uncoupled Hessian is $\operatorname{diag}(2,4)$. Let $v=(1,-1)^T$. The last square contributes the outer product $vv^T$ to the Hessian, giving the matrix used above. With the coupling removed the energy quotient is a weighted average of $2$ and $4$, so the smallest value is $2$ on the first coordinate axis.

### B. Find a soft integer direction

Find an integer command with both entries positive and quotient below $2.7$.

**Solution.** Scaling preserves the quotient. Use $(2,1)^T$. The numerator is $3\cdot4-4+5=13$, the denominator is $5$, and $13/5=2.6$.

### C. Complex phase and energy

Put $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,i)$ and $G=DHD^*$. Find the off-diagonal entries of $G$ and one soft mode from an old soft mode $x$.

**Solution.** The entries are $g_{12}=i$ and $g_{21}=-i$. Since $D^*D=I_2$, $G(Dx)=D(Hx)$. Thus $Dx$ is a soft eigenvector with the same eigenvalue. The real-coordinate quotient bounds extend directly to this Hermitian phase convention.

### D. A quotient inside a gap

Find a unit vector with quotient exactly $4$. Does the value certify a mode at $4$?

**Solution.** If $u_1,u_2$ are normalized eigenvectors, $(u_1+u_2)/\sqrt2$ gives weight $1/2$ to each eigenvalue. Its quotient is $4$. The residual norm at that value is $\sqrt2$, the distance from $4$ to either eigenvalue. The quotient records the mean of mode stiffnesses for this input.

### E. A repeated eigenvalue

For $H=\operatorname{diag}(6,6,1)$, what is the smallest possible maximum quotient after imposing one homogeneous orthogonality constraint?

**Solution.** The allowed plane intersects the two-dimensional eigenvalue-$6$ subspace in at least a line. The quotient equals $6$ on that line and is at most $6$ everywhere. Thus every such plane gives maximum $6$. One constraint cannot remove a mode of multiplicity two.
